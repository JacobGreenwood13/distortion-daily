Outrage autopsy № A6

Capitalizing On Outrage

A two-hour conversation goes into the editing room. Twenty minutes of confrontation comes out. The confrontation gets sliced into thirty seconds. The thirty seconds get fed to ten million feeds before dinner.

By Jacob · Editor · November 2, 2026 · 9 min read

A two-hour conversation goes into the editing room. Twenty minutes of confrontation comes out. The confrontation gets sliced into thirty seconds. The thirty seconds get fed to ten million feeds before dinner.

Nobody in those ten million feeds chose this. The choosing happened upstream, in rooms you will never see, by people whose names you will never know.

The gotcha moment — the clip where somebody gets owned, exposed, destroyed, left speechless — looks like an accident of debate. It is the product: manufactured, packaged, and sold. The product has buyers on every side. You are not one of them.

THE PIPELINE

Jubilee Media runs the most successful debate factory on YouTube: over 10.8 million subscribers. Their flagship format, Surrounded, drops one prominent figure into a circle of twenty or more opponents: they rush, musical-chairs style, for a single seat across the table, and the ones on the perimeter raise red flags when they feel each exchange has run its course.

The company's own creative director says two-hour conversations get cut down to twenty minutes or less. The company's leadership judges every format on criteria that include, in their own words, whether it can lead to viral moments. Episodes ship under titles engineered as partisan bait. And the confrontational peaks — the "owns" — get relentlessly reposted across TikTok, Instagram, and X as snippets of partisan victory. That last description comes from Fast Company, not from us.

Two hours in, twenty minutes out, thirty seconds everywhere. At every step, the calm parts get cut and the conflict parts get kept. The founder wants to build "the Disney for empathy" — the company's stated aim is to "provoke understanding and create human connection." The conversation happened. The editing room decides what the table looked like.

WHY CONFLICT PAYS

This is the documented business model of the platforms, confirmed by their own people and their own researchers.

Peer-reviewed research on more than half a million social media messages found that every additional moral-emotional word in a post about a contested issue increased its spread by roughly twenty percent. A second study, funded by the National Science Foundation, ran preregistered experiments on thousands of users and found that people rewarded with likes and retweets for expressing outrage went on to express more outrage — a reinforcement loop — and that politically moderate users were the most sensitive to the rewards. The platforms train the behavior the way a slot machine trains a gambler.

The platforms' own documents say the same thing in corporate language. Meta's published ranking documentation states that its models predict how likely a user is to comment on a post and how likely the post is to spark back-and-forth discussion. Translation: the feed ranks by predicted argument. YouTube's vice president of engineering wrote publicly that recommendations — driven by clicks, views, watch time, and shares — account for more viewership than subscriptions or search combined.

Then the whistleblowers. Frances Haugen, the former Facebook product manager who testified to the U.S. Senate, said the company's internal research showed it is easier to inspire people to anger than to any other emotion — and that making the algorithm safer would mean people spend less time on the site and click fewer ads. The Wall Street Journal's reporting on internal Facebook research found the company's own scientists warning that its algorithms exploit the human brain's attraction to divisiveness, and that nearly two-thirds of the people who joined German extremist groups did so through Facebook's recommendation tools. Executives sidelined proposed fixes, the Journal reported, partly over fears of looking biased against conservatives. Facebook's integrity chief disputed the reporting on the record. Both claims are on the table; the internal documents exist either way.

The platforms do not all tilt the same way. X tilts right: a randomized Nature experiment on nearly five thousand users found its algorithmic feed boosted right-leaning content, cut traditional news outlets' visibility by more than half, elevated political activists over journalists, and moved users toward Republican-favored issue priorities — and the shift stuck even after users switched back to a chronological feed. An earlier study found the same rightward amplification on pre-Musk Twitter across six of seven countries. Facebook and Instagram run the colder version. Their feeds reinforce whatever bubble the user already sits in — liberals get liberal rage, conservatives get conservative rage — and amplify outrage because outrage holds attention. Per the company's own whistleblower, anger is the easiest emotion to provoke, and safer algorithms would cost the company attention and advertising clicks. Remember the rest of the record: this is the company that let a political consultancy siphon the data of up to 87 million users through a personality-quiz app, then ate a $5 billion FTC fine — the largest privacy penalty in the agency's history — and posted strong earnings the same day. They don't care about you. You're nothing but a number to them. Every second you spend there? Their profits rise. Your attention is their currency. Their incentive is to keep you glued to their site. Keep you divided. Meanwhile? Their profits rise. Our read: Facebook and Instagram lean left. The published studies measure engagement mechanics, not partisan allegiance, so they won't hand you that sentence — but the pattern of what gets amplified, what gets throttled, and whose outrage gets monetized points one way. The large Meta-run 2020 experiments showed the machine's real loyalty: swapping the algorithmic feed for a chronological one produced no detectable change in users' political attitudes. The algorithm leaves your beliefs alone. It keeps you furious, keeps you scrolling, keeps you selling.

THE NEUTRALITY COSTUME

Jubilee's founder says the company features voices from both sides as equally and accurately as possible, hires producers and editors with a variety of viewpoints, and applies a balance test: equal cancellation from both sides means the work is unbiased.

The criticism comes from both directions, on the record. From the left: a dozen named critics — writers, activists, former guests — accuse the company of platforming far-right figures for rage-bait, of casting career debaters against ordinary participants, of designing the formats so the most inflammatory voices travel furthest. One prominent guest said the company was deliberate about inviting provocateurs because it knew the result would go viral. A well-known media critic called the output performative middle-ground theater built to feed algorithms that circulate whatever inflames.

From the right: a conservative creator who debated on the channel twice said the on-screen fact-checking banners appeared only against her side, never the other, and that unrelated statistics were displayed to create the appearance of debunking.

Strip away the dueling accusations and what remains is the structure: a company that claims neutrality, engineers its formats for viral confrontation, compresses hours of conversation into minutes of conflict, and feeds a clip economy where each side harvests its own thirty seconds of victory. The neutrality is the costume. The confrontation is the product.

THE MONEY

Now the money. Because the gotcha pipeline does not run on ad revenue alone. It runs on patronage — and the patronage comes from political machines on both sides, mostly in the dark.

Start with the foreign case, because it is the best documented. In September 2024, the Department of Justice unsealed an indictment charging two employees of Russia's state media apparatus with funneling roughly $9.7 million through shell companies into a Tennessee media company identified in reporting as Tenet Media — nearly ninety percent of the company's bank deposits over ten months. The money paid right-wing commentators for thousands of videos. The commentators named in reporting were not charged and were not accused of wrongdoing; the indictment describes them as deceived about the funding source, and they described themselves as victims. The two Russians remain at large. The company is dead. The playbook is public.

The domestic left runs the same playbook with domestic money. A Democratic dark-money network's project recruited more than ninety Democratic-aligned influencers, paying up to $8,000 a month to promote party messaging — under contracts that barred participants from disclosing the arrangement. The House Oversight Committee subpoenaed the fund behind it in May 2026. A separate Democratic-aligned venture paid more than a hundred micro-creators thirty dollars a video to post scripted "affordability crisis" content ahead of the midterms; investigators found personas that did not match the people posting — a "Texas born and raised" creator posting from England. A Soros-family fund put $15 million in a single year into a network of eleven swing-state "local news" outlets that outside raters flagged for undisclosed partisan financing. On the right, a network of roughly thirteen hundred partisan local-news lookalike sites drew more than $14 million from PACs and nonprofits traceable to conservative donor networks, plus a $75 million war chest behind a companion operation.

The cleanest documented case of cash for positions is almost petty enough to be funny. In March 2025, a PR firm reportedly offered MAGA-aligned accounts up to $1,000 per post — templates included — to oppose a plan to remove soda from food assistance. Multiple accounts posted near-identical talking points and abruptly reversed their stated views. One prominent account holder said she was offered the money and declined. Another apologized publicly. None of the paid posts disclosed the payment. The beverage lobby denied involvement.

The evidence shows pay for positions: specific stances, specific posts, specific checks. The receipts show positions rented by the post — a rental is pure inventory where a conversion would at least be a belief. A thousand dollars a post, templates included.

THE DARK

Here is the number the editor asked us to carry: nine out of ten. Nine out of ten political creators who stay in the game end up on a party's payroll — or performing for one, which amounts to the same thing.

That number is ours. No survey measures it. No longitudinal study tracks creator alignment over time. No dataset counts what share of political creators take patronage money, because the payments are systematically undisclosed — which is itself the central finding of every watchdog that has looked. The closest published figure cuts the other way: a Pew Research Center study of five hundred news influencers found barely half publicly express any political orientation at all.

Nine in ten is our estimate, not a finding. The estimate stands on this: Federal law requires disclosure of paid political influencer content nowhere — the endorsement rules cover commercial products, and the proposal to extend them to politics died without a vote. A federal bill to fix it has not gotten a vote either. Two states have acted: California, with a weak 2023 law and a stronger fines bill still moving, and Texas, with an ethics commission rule from 2024. Everywhere else, the transaction is legal, invisible, and routine. Campaigns spent nearly two billion dollars on online political advertising in the 2024 cycle, and the watchdogs tracking that figure note explicitly that it does not include payments to influencers — because nobody is required to report them.

The system is designed to be uncountable. When the money must hide to work, the hiding is the work.

THE VERDICT

The gotcha is inventory — manufactured by formats engineered for confrontation, amplified by platforms optimized for anger, purchased by political machines that rent positions by the post, and laundered through disclosure laws that do not exist.

Everyone in the pipeline gets paid except you. You get the thirty seconds. You share the thirty seconds. You argue about the thirty seconds. Your anger is the product being sold, your share is the distribution network, and your vote is the thing being harvested at the end of the season.

The defense is pattern recognition. When a clip makes your blood move before your mind does, check the pipeline: who filmed it, who cut it, who clipped it, who paid for the account that posted it. Two hours went in. You are watching thirty seconds. Somebody chose those thirty seconds, and somebody paid for the choosing.

You are the inventory.
