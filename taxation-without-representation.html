Taxation Without Representation

On December 16, 1773, colonists dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor over taxation without representation. The national debt is now $40.1 trillion — $117,279 per American — run up by representatives picked through a donor filter most voters never see. The form changed. The grievance didn’t.

By Jacob · Editor · October 12, 2026 · 14 min read

On December 16, 1773, colonists boarded three ships in Boston Harbor and dumped 342 chests of tea into the water.

They weren't angry about tea. They were angry about the principle: a Parliament across the ocean — one they didn't elect, couldn't vote out, and had no voice in — was taxing them anyway. That grievance started a revolution.

They won the war. Then the new country spent 250 years building a machine that does the same thing with extra steps.

This is that story, from 1773 to now. The wars. The tax schemes. The trade deals. And the $40.1 trillion receipt sitting on your table [1].

THE MENU AND THE BILL

Here's how it actually works. Forget the civics-class diagram.

You don't pick the candidates. Donors do — before you ever vote. Running for federal office costs millions. Long before Election Day, the parties (through their campaign arms: the DCCC, NRCC, DSCC, NRSC) recruit candidates, direct money, and shape primaries. Wealthy donors, PACs, and industry groups decide who's "viable." You choose from a menu. Someone else writes it.

The leash stays on after they win. A representative who bucks the party's big donors can watch their funding dry up, find a well-funded primary challenger, lose their committee seats. Nobody has to make a threat. Everyone understands how the money moves.

Your representative ends up with two bosses: the voters back home, and the funders everywhere else. The career depends on re-election. Re-election depends on money. And the money comes from a tiny slice of the population — not from voters.

Follow the money, step by step:

  • Who decides: party leadership and major donors.
  • What they get: control over who governs, without ever appearing on a ballot.
  • How: campaign cash as the gatekeeper before the election, and the leash after it.
  • Who pays: you — the taxpayer funding the government they run.
  • Who takes the risk: you — the voter who gets the bill but never wrote the menu.

The colonists' complaint was simple: we're taxed by people we can't remove. The modern version: you're taxed — and borrowed against — by people you technically can remove, except every replacement comes from the same menu, funded by the same donors. The form changed. The grievance didn't.

THE WARS: every one since 1890, with the math shown

All dollar amounts are converted to today's dollars so old wars can be compared fairly. The math is shown line by line so you can check it.

How to read the verdicts: "Choice" means we chose it — no attack on America forced it, the excuse fell apart, or there was another way. "Contested" means honest people disagree, so it's flagged, not forced. "Necessary" wars are left out of the indictment — because a ledger that can't name a necessary war can't be trusted to name an unnecessary one.

The post-9/11 wars (2001–2022) — VERDICT: CHOICE

Afghanistan, Iraq, Syria, Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia, and the rest. Iraq's WMD excuse was disproven. Afghanistan's retaliation turned into 20 years of nation-building nobody voted for.

What that means for you:

$8.0T out of $40.1T = 20% → one dollar in five of the national debt is these wars
$8.0T ÷ 340 million Americans = $23,529 per person
$6.5T in future interest ÷ 340 million = $19,118 more per person — just for the borrowing
Combined: $42,647 per American. For the financing alone. [2]

Started on the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution — passed over reports of attacks that were disputed then and discredited later.

Cost in today's dollars ........................................... $738 billion
Paid for mostly with deficits. Still owed today: ~$0 (paid off or inflated away over 50 years)

Korea (1950–1953) — VERDICT: CONTESTED

A "police action" under the UN. No declaration of war. Flagged, not forced.

Cost in today's dollars ........................................... $341 billion
Paid for almost entirely with TAXES — Truman raised them to cover it.
Still owed today: ~$0

This one matters: America knew how to pay for wars. It chose to stop.

World War I (1917–1921) — VERDICT: CONTESTED

Real provocations (submarine attacks, the Zimmermann telegram). Whether we had to jump in is still debated by historians.

Cost: $33 billion back then → $334 billion in today's dollars
Paid for ~30% with taxes, ~70% with war bonds. Still owed today: ~$0

The Spanish-American War (1898) + Philippine-American War (1899–1902) — VERDICT: CHOICE

The explosion of the USS Maine became the excuse. Then America bought the Philippines from Spain — and crushed the Filipinos' own independence movement.

  — for a nation whose people were never asked. They'd declared

Filipino civilians killed: over 200,000 (some estimates far higher)
Still owed today: ~$0

The first war to force American rule onto a foreign people who had already freed themselves. The template for every "spreading democracy" war since.

The Gulf War (1990–1991) — VERDICT: CONTESTED

A real coalition liberated Kuwait. Oil interests lurked in the background. But look at the numbers — this is the one that indicts everything after it:

Total cost ........................................................ $102 billion
Allies paid back .................................................. ~$97.3 billion
ACTUAL COST TO US TAXPAYERS ....................................... $4.7 billion
  (Allies covered 92.3%. We paid 7.7%.)

Sharing the burden was possible. Every war since was a choice to pay full price alone.

Kosovo (1999) — VERDICT: CHOICE

78 days of NATO bombing. No declaration of war. Congress never even voted. (Exact cost still being sourced — estimated under $5 billion.)

Libya (2011) — VERDICT: CHOICE

No vote in Congress. Regime change sold as "protecting civilians."

US cost, March–September 2011 ..................................... ~$1.1 billion
The result: Gaddafi dead, the country shattered, no US embassy since.

Left out: World War II ($4.1–4.7 trillion in today's dollars)

A defensive war after a direct attack on America. About half paid with taxes. Necessary — excluded.

THE MONEY WE LENT AND NEVER GOT BACK

In World War I, nearly a third of what America spent wasn't spent on our own troops at all — it was loaned to our allies:

$9.5 billion lent ÷ $33 billion total = 28.8% — nearly a third of the bill was loans to other countries

Most of it was never repaid. Britain and France defaulted during the Great Depression. America borrowed the money, lent it abroad, and swallowed the loss — plus interest, for decades.

And the pattern never stopped. Reconstruction funds. Military aid. Nation-building budgets. Borrowed in Washington, spent in foreign capitals, repaid by no one.

You get charged twice: once for the war, once for the loan that paid for someone else's war.

REAGANOMICS: how taxes got rigged and the debt tripled

Wars are half the receipt. The other half is the tax system the donor filter built for itself.

In 1981, President Reagan signed the Economic Recovery Tax Act — the biggest tax cut of the era. The top tax rate (the rate the richest pay on their highest dollars of income) was slashed from 70% to 50%, then down to 28% by 1986. The sales pitch, called "trickle-down" economics: cut taxes at the top, and the growth will pay for itself.

It didn't. Here's the math:

National debt, 1982 ............................................ ~$1.1 trillion
National debt, 1989 ............................................ ~$2.9 trillion
                                                            ─────────────
$2.9T ÷ $1.1T = nearly TRIPLE — in seven years

Congress panicked so fast it partially undid the cuts the very next year. And in 2012, the government's own non-partisan researchers studied 70 years of tax rates and concluded: cutting top tax rates does nothing measurable for economic growth or productivity — it just increases inequality [3].

Their own researchers said the promise was empty. The policy stayed anyway. And every president since has run the same play: cut taxes (donors cheer), don't cut spending (donors would boo), borrow the difference — and mail the bill to people who weren't in the room.

That's taxation without representation in its purest form. Not a tax you didn't vote for — a debt you didn't vote for, run up by representatives who passed the donor filter, for the benefit of the donors who funded them.

THE CAR BUSINESS: the $3.3 trillion bleed

Now the part nobody puts on the receipt: how American industry got hollowed out — and who signed the papers.

America's share of cars built worldwide:
  1965 ............................................................ 46%
  Today ............................................................. 14.7%

The Big Three's share of cars sold in America (GM, Ford, Stellantis):
  1965 ............................................................ 92%
  Today ............................................................. 38%

Total auto trade deficit, 1963–2023 ............................... $3.3 trillion [4]

(That last number means: over 60 years, America bought $3.3 trillion more in cars and parts from other countries than it sold to them. That's money that left.)

This wasn't bad luck. It was policy:

  • Trade deals written with corporations at the table. The tariff rules and trade agreements were negotiated by representatives funded by the same multinational companies that then moved factories abroad. The worker was never in the room. The corporation never left it.
  • American companies offshoring, then selling back to us. GM — an American company — now imports nearly 1.2 million more vehicles into the US than it exports. Ford imports more than it exports too. They didn't just lose to foreign competition. They joined it: move the factory to Mexico for cheaper labor, sell the cars back to the Americans whose jobs you moved.
  • The jobs, in plain numbers: American manufacturing employed 19.4 million people in 1979. By 2010, fewer than 11.5 million. It's crawled back to about 13 million — still near the lowest since before World War II. Manufacturing went from 1 in 3 American jobs (1953) to fewer than 1 in 12 today [5].

In 2024, foreign car companies built 4.9 million vehicles inside the United States — more than the Big Three's 4.6 million [4]. The industry didn't die. It just stopped being American in any way that matters to the American worker.

The CEO who moves a plant to Mexico gets a bonus. The worker who loses the job gets a pamphlet. The representative who approved the trade deal gets re-elected — with corporate money. Everyone in the deal consented. Except the people it was done to.

SO WHAT ACTUALLY BROKE THE ECONOMY?

Not one thing. Anyone selling you a single cause is selling you something. It's the machine — every part feeding the same result:

  1. Wars on the credit card — $8 trillion for the post-9/11 wars, $14.5 trillion with interest. Ordered by filtered representatives. Billed to people not yet born.
  2. Tax cuts for the donor class — top rate 70% → 28%. Revenue gutted. Debt tripled in seven years. The government's own researchers admitted it didn't grow the economy.
  3. Trade deals for the corporations — $3.3 trillion auto deficit, 6.5 million manufacturing jobs gone. Written with industry in the room and workers outside it.
  4. The borrowing that ties it all together — cut taxes and keep spending and fight wars and let factories leave, and the only way the math works is debt. Debt is the perfect crime: the people who benefit vote today, and the people who pay can't — they're not born yet, they're too broke to matter, or they live in a country we bombed.

The common thread isn't a party. Both parties did this. The common thread is the filter: every one of these decisions was made by people picked through the donor menu — and every bill was sent to people who never meaningfully agreed.

The colonists dumped the tea because they refused to pay for what they didn't agree to. Today, every American owes $117,279 — for wars, tax schemes, and trade deals most of them were never meaningfully offered. The whole $40.1 trillion is the harbor. We're all standing on the dock, watching it sink.

THE RECEIPT, ALL IN ONE PLACE

  • Post-9/11 wars still on the books: $8 trillion — one dollar in five of the entire debt.
  • Interest on war borrowing: $925 billion so far, $6.5 trillion projected by 2050. Lifetime war cost: $14.5 trillion.
  • Reagan-era tax cuts: debt nearly tripled in seven years. The template ever since.
  • Auto trade deficit since 1963: $3.3 trillion. Manufacturing jobs: 19.4 million → ~13 million.
  • This year through August: the deficit hit ~$1.97 trillion; interest payments alone passed ~$1.02 trillion. Over a trillion a year just to service the debt — paying for the borrowing, not for anything built.

WHAT WOULD ACTUAL CONSENT LOOK LIKE?

The fix targets the machine — not a party, not a person. The incentives:

  1. No war without Congress. The Constitution says Congress declares war. Enforce it — or admit that clause is decoration. No more blank-check authorizations stretched across decades.
  2. No war without paying for it. An automatic war tax, so taxpayers feel the cost while the war is happening and can object in real time. Korea proved it works. The post-9/11 method proved what happens without it.
  3. Break the donor filter. Public campaign financing, contribution limits with teeth — or at minimum, real-time disclosure, so voters can see the second boss giving the orders.
  4. Workers at the trade table. No more deals negotiated by the corporations that profit from offshoring while the workers getting offshored wait outside.
  5. Real choices on the ballot. Ballot and debate rules that don't let two parties write the menu and call it democracy.

Starting points, not a platform. The details are yours to draft.

They dumped the tea because they refused to pay for what they didn't agree to. The question was never whether that principle was right — the country was founded on it. The question is whether it still applies.

The ledger is open.
