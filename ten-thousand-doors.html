Ten Thousand Doors

By Jacob · Editor · October 10, 2026 · 7 min read

You were 32. You ran because the rent went up $400 and your sister called you crying — she didn't know where her kids would sleep come October, in the neighborhood you both grew up in. Then the eviction notice went up on the building down the street. Then your neighbor. You knocked on ten thousand doors and every one of them opened onto some version of your own life. You believed — God help you, you actually believed — that if you got inside the government building, you could fix it. You were once a bright eyed kid with hopes and dreams. You clung to your ideals, even got your butt kicked a few times over your devotion to them. Now you're older, more experienced, went to grad school, built up a solid network that said they could help you out.

Before you picked a party, you tried not to. You told yourself you wouldn't be owned by either of them due to the extremism of the current political climate and how the dominant parties have a proclivity to always focus on certain problems, but in the over 4000 years of human civilization, NEVER have they come up with a permanent solution. So your mind was made up. Run independent, answer to nobody, slip your conscience into the candidacy form. You had faith that the American people would see what you yourself have seen to be the real issue. Polarization. Centralization of power amidst the dominant political parties.

Then a consultant friend did the math on a napkin: without running as part of the duopoly, that meant no party money, no party voter file, no party volunteers, no debate stage that matters. "You can do it," she said. "You'll get four percent of the vote and a great story." In your head... you couldn't help thinking "Is four percent really enough?" Your whole life everyone has said the same thing in different words: there are really two choices. The ballot laws help make it true. The money makes it permanent.

So you held your breath and picked a side reluctantly, knowing that this is the pre-mortem equivalent to selling your immortal soul. But not to a devil. Something far more sinister and far less self-aware... To man. It was the only viable business decision, and you knew it.

The committee called before you'd even announced. "We've been watching your race." They had polling. They had a plan. They had money. You won the people's vote...

You did the homework. It wasn't complicated. There weren't enough homes. The economists you read all pointed the same way: let people build where people want to live. You drafted the bill yourself, late nights, your aide asleep on the office couch. It was clean. It would have worked. You could see your sister's street in every line of it. The woman in the office next door had her own version of a bill like that. Hers died in February.

Then they called you in. Not a hearing — a conference room. Friendly smiles. Coffee. The man across the table had been in Washington longer than you'd been alive, and he told you the bill was dead. He wasn't cruel about it. He'd given this speech a hundred times, and for a brief moment, you actually believed he was saving you — from yourself, from a short career, from becoming a cautionary tale even sooner.

Nobody in that room discussed whether the bill was good. They laughed and brushed it off, acting as though it was cute, like some kind of 3 month old puppy, that the new politician was fighting for his sister to have a place to live. Then, it happened... a man in glasses smiled the devil's own and declared, to your horror: "A solved housing crisis doesn't raise money. An unsolved one raises millions and provides an incentive for the American people to rely on us. — We rely on them to rely on us. It's how we receive votes. — Every cycle, both parties send fundraising emails with your district's pain in the subject line. Your bill would fix the problem, which in turn kill the incentive for them to vote for us. And the fix would get you killed back home: the homeowners who vote in every primary like their equity climbing, the donors like scarcity, and the other party already had the bulldozer ad cut and waiting." You didn't blame the homeowners, either. Their house was their retirement. The system taught them to guard it the way it taught you to guard your seat.

Then he slid a folder across the table. A different bill. Big patriotic title. Built to die in committee and live on cable news. "You'll get your TV hit," he said. You took the folder. Don't lie — you know you would have. The mortgage. The staffers. The four hours a day on the phone begging strangers for money — they call it "call time," and it's the real job. The career you'd just started.

Now, the people's vote? Won. However, your bill made you a loose cannon in their eyes. They had a network built over decades within the committee rooms. They didn't need to buy a single vote. The chairman buried your bill without a hearing. Leadership kept it off the calendar. The whip made sure nobody broke ranks to force it out. Your bill never got a vote. It died in a drawer, the way most of them do.

Still, despite that, the money was good. You thought to yourself "Well I couldn't fix the block but I can at least buy my sister a new house with this kind of income." So you stayed silent. You cashed the paycheck. You read the talking points. You told yourself you'd change things from the inside, and the inside kept shrinking until you couldn't find the person who knocked on those ten thousand doors anymore. Little by little, you felt less and less like the man you once were. You grew compromised...

Then? Tragedy struck... As you were signing your sea of endless papers, skimming through new policies, you receive a letter. A letter from the county. Your heart sank as you read the contents of the letter. Through tear-streaked eyes, you felt an unimaginable, unbridled rage. The letter brought this as news: As a result of their new bill — the one from the folder — federal money flowed into a "revitalization grant," and the city used it to condemn the entire block. Unbeknownst to you, your sister had stayed behind, as she had nowhere else to turn. It's a small town. The people were forgotten here, so there were no women's shelters for her to turn to. As a result of your endless campaigns and long nights away from home, she lost all hope... She stayed inside as the bulldozer struck her house... Her bloody mangled corpse was flattened completely and that was all that was left of her...

You read that letter until the words blurred. And something in you snapped — not broke. Snapped, like a bone resetting wrong. Every year of silence, every cashed check, every talking point you read with a straight face, every night you told yourself you'd fix it from the inside — it all caught fire at once.

You gave the speech. The real one. Not on their cable channels — they wouldn't book you for this. On the House floor, into the congressional record where they can't edit you out. You named the conference room. You named the folder. You named the man in the glasses and his sermon about how they rely on us to rely on them. You held up the bill that would have worked in one hand and the bill that flattened your sister in the other, and you told the American people exactly what the difference was.

And the machine did exactly what it said it would do. Your committee seats were gone by Friday. The donors stopped calling by Monday. By the end of the month, the money that used to be yours had found your primary challenger — some bright-eyed 32-year-old knocking on ten thousand doors, actually believing he could fix it. The bookers forgot your number.

You lost everything. The career. The house. The seat at the table. But that night you slept. Because for the first time in years, the man who knocked on those ten thousand doors was back in the room — and he wasn't quiet anymore.
