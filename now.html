The Now

The week in federal and global affairs — what happened, the incentives in play, and the sources. Refreshed regularly, never quietly rewritten.

1. Shutdown reaches day 27 as November food assistance stops and states sue over contingency funds

What happened: On October 27, the federal government shutdown reached its 27th day, the second longest in U.S. history, according to the National Low Income Housing Coalition. Congress has not passed funding bills for fiscal year 2026, which began on October 1, or a continuing resolution to temporarily maintain funding. Democrats are withholding support for a continuing resolution to press for an extension of Affordable Care Act tax credits set to expire at the end of the year; open enrollment for 2026 coverage begins on November 1, and without an extension, annual premium payments are expected to increase by 114 percent, or $1,016 on average, for subsidized enrollees. An estimated 1.4 million federal employees did not receive a paycheck last week. The Agriculture Department's Food and Nutrition Service told states to hold off distributing November Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits until further notice, and 25 states told Politico they are issuing notices telling participants they will not receive checks next month, including California, Arkansas, Hawaii, Indiana, Mississippi, and New Jersey. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins said, "We just can't do it without the government being open," and said the administration is hopeful the government reopens by November 1. The program serves about 42 million people and costs about $8 billion a month. On October 27, attorneys general and governors from 25 states and the District of Columbia sued the Agriculture Department in federal court in Boston, arguing the department should use about $6 billion in contingency funds to pay November benefits; U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani set a hearing for October 29 on a temporary restraining order. The department updated its website on October 24 to say no benefits would be issued on November 1, stating "the well has run dry." November 1 is also the first day millions of households receiving the Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program will not receive benefits, according to the National Low Income Housing Coalition.

Incentives:

  • Congress: the documented mechanism is the 60-vote Senate threshold for a continuing resolution, which lets a minority block funding until its condition is met; the stated Democratic condition is the health-care tax credit extension.
  • The Agriculture Department: the documented mechanisms are the hold-off instruction to states and the contingency-fund decision, which together determine whether November benefits are issued during the lapse.
  • The suing states: the documented mechanism is a federal lawsuit asking a judge to order the use of contingency funds, with a hearing set for October 29.
  • Structural note: the November 1 cutoff is the department's stated position as of October 27, and the lawsuit had not been decided when this edition was published, so the outcome for November benefits was still open.

Sources:

  • National Low Income Housing Coalition, October 27 — day 27, missed paychecks, premium increase, November 1 cutoff
  • National Low Income Housing Coalition, October 27 — SNAP and heating assistance, 42 million recipients
  • Politico reporting quoted, October 27 — 25 states issuing notices, Rollins statement
  • Produce Market Guide, October 27 — states' lawsuit, $6 billion contingency fund, October 29 hearing

2. Oleshky humanitarian ceasefire takes effect on October 27 while strikes continue elsewhere

What happened: The local ceasefire in Oleshky, a Russian-controlled town in southern Ukraine, took effect on October 27, according to statements from both countries. Russia's Defence Ministry said on Telegram it stopped shelling toward the Ukrainian-held city of Kherson to prepare for the humanitarian effort in Oleshky, which faces Kherson across the Dnipro River. Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine's parliamentary commissioner for human rights and head of its negotiating team, confirmed in a Facebook post that the Ukrainian side is honoring its commitments, and said the deal was reached in coordination with U.S. First Lady Melania Trump and with Yana Lantratova, head of the Russian negotiating team. The White House said on October 23 that Trump served as the "exclusive intermediary" in the arrangement. The ceasefire is restricted to the Oleshky area and is intended to allow deliveries of food, drinking water, and medicine, followed by evacuation routes for residents who wish to leave. Up to 2,000 civilians remain in Oleshky, according to United Nations estimates cited by the Associated Press, many of them elderly or with limited mobility. The ceasefire does not apply to the wider war: on October 27, strikes hit the Southern Bridge and subway infrastructure in Kyiv, forcing a suspension of traffic, and a Reuters witness heard explosions. The October 26 Russian accusation that Ukraine struck Oleshky 18 times with artillery and carried out more than 120 drone attacks since October 19 remains unverified by Reuters, with no immediate Ukrainian response reported.

Incentives:

  • Ukraine and Russia: the documented mechanism is the local ceasefire and its aid and evacuation corridors, confined to the Oleshky area rather than the wider front.
  • The intermediaries: the White House statement names the First Lady as "exclusive intermediary," and Lubinets names Lantratova as the Russian counterpart in the negotiation.
  • Structural note: the White House's original October 23 statement listed October 29 as the starting date, while Russian and Ukrainian officials confirmed October 27; the reporting does not explain the difference.

Sources:

  • Reuters, October 27 — ceasefire start, shelling halt, Lubinets confirmation, Kyiv strikes
  • Universaladage, October 27 — scope of the ceasefire, 2,000 civilians, October 29 date difference
  • Crisis Zone, October 27 — White House intermediary statement, Kyiv casualties

3. Israel votes October 27 and wakes to no parliamentary majority

What happened: Israel held a general election on October 27 for all 120 seats in the Knesset, with 61 seats needed for a majority. Turnout was 77.7 percent, the highest since 1999, according to CNN. With 70.7 percent of votes tallied on the morning of October 28, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc of right-wing, far-right, and ultra-Orthodox parties was forecast to win 54 seats, the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot was forecast to win 47 seats, and parties representing Israelis of Arab descent were forecast to win 19 seats, according to Reuters. Neither major bloc reached a majority. Netanyahu remains prime minister as caretaker during coalition negotiations, or until a new election in 2027 if no government is formed. On October 28, Netanyahu told Likud supporters he would seek "a strong, right-wing national government" and raised the prospect of a renewed joint military action against Iran alongside U.S. President Donald Trump. Eisenkot said Netanyahu had lost public support, urged him to retire from politics, and warned that Netanyahu might use security for political ends. Definitive results were expected on October 29. On October 28, Israeli markets moved against the partial results: the shekel weakened about 2 percent to 3.1185 to the dollar, Tel Aviv's TA125 index fell about 3 percent, the yield on the benchmark domestic 10-year bond rose about 5 basis points to 4.13 percent, and Brent crude traded at $115 a barrel, up 1 percent.

Incentives:

  • The blocs: the documented mechanism is coalition arithmetic in a 120-seat parliament — 54 plus 19 reaches 73, and 47 plus 19 reaches 66, so either bloc needs Arab-party backing to reach 61, while Arab parties have ruled out some partnerships and opposition allies have ruled out others.
  • The caretaker government: Netanyahu stays in office during negotiations, which keeps executive authority with the incumbent while talks continue.
  • Markets: the shekel, stock index, and bond yield figures are the measured market response to the partial results, not a verdict on any eventual coalition.
  • Structural note: the seat figures are projections from a partial count, and the opposition total differs between outlets at different count stages, so the final distribution can still change.

Sources:

  • Reuters, October 28 — 54-47-19 projection, caretaker status, Netanyahu and Eisenkot statements, oil price
  • Reuters, October 28 — market moves, definitive results expected October 29
  • Le Monde, October 28 — turnout, outgoing coalition seat loss, Arab-party gains
  • CNN, October 27 — 77.7 percent turnout, Arab-party surge, coalition obstacles

4. Alaska judge expedites the certification lawsuit and signals skepticism, with an intervention ruling due October 28

What happened: On October 26, U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason granted expedited consideration in the Justice Department's lawsuit seeking to block Alaska from certifying its November 3 U.S. Senate election result if voter confusion over two candidates named Sullivan changes the outcome. Gleason directed the department to respond to the motions to intervene by October 27, allowed replies by October 28, and said she intends to rule on the intervention motions by October 28. On October 27, the department filed its opposition to the motions to intervene, and the court granted the Alaska election boards' motion for expedited consideration; the department must respond to the motion to dismiss by October 29. The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and the campaign committee of Democratic challenger Mary Peltola sought to intervene and obtain dismissal before Election Day, arguing that leaving the case unresolved would let it hang like a "Sword of Damocles" over the election; voter organizations and two individual voters also sought intervention, and Alaska filed its own dismissal motion. In her first order acknowledging the case, Gleason highlighted the last-minute nature of the government's action, noting that Alaskans had already begun casting ballots and that nearly four months had passed since the Alaska Supreme Court directed the Division of Elections to include Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. on the ballot. The suit, filed October 22, asks the court to block certification of any result that "may be impacted by voter confusion," pending an investigation into whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant for incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan.

Incentives:

  • The court: the documented mechanism is the expedited briefing schedule, which forces intervention and dismissal questions to be decided before Election Day rather than after certification.
  • The intervenors: intervention would give the campaign committees, voter organizations, and individual voters standing to argue for dismissal in a case filed against the state.
  • The Justice Department: the filing asks a court to hold certification while votes are investigated; a filing does not itself block certification, and no ruling on dismissal had been issued when this edition was published.
  • Structural note: the October 28 ruling Gleason intends to issue covers intervention, not the merits of the certification request.

Sources:

  • Democracy Docket, October 27 — case timeline, October 27 opposition, October 29 dismissal deadline
  • ABC News via KTBB, October 27 — Gleason order, last-minute timing, ballot status
  • Washington Examiner, October 27 — intervention requests, "Sword of Damocles" argument, briefing schedule

5. Boston judge blocks the rule cutting refugees' time to contest loss of status to 10 days

What happened: On October 27, U.S. District Judge Julia Kobick in Boston temporarily blocked the administration from implementing a rule issued by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services on October 14 that would cut the time refugees have to contest decisions stripping them of legal status to 10 days from 30 days. Kobick's order puts the rule on hold through November 6 to preserve the status quo until the case can be heard by a colleague. Refugee advocacy groups sought the order as part of a lawsuit filed on October 26 challenging the rule and a plan for a broader review of refugees. The Department of Homeland Security, which oversees Citizenship and Immigration Services, had estimated the rule would affect as many as 186,000 refugees. Erez Reuveni of Democracy Forward, a lawyer for the plaintiffs, said the decision "provides meaningful protection for people who were already facing termination notices." Citizenship and Immigration Services spokesperson Zach Kahler called the lawsuit a "frivolous attempt to disrupt the Trump administration's commitment to enforce our country's immigration laws as intended by Congress." The Department of Homeland Security did not respond to a request for comment.

Incentives:

  • The court: the documented mechanism is a temporary hold through November 6, which keeps the 30-day window in place while the challenge is heard.
  • The administration: the documented mechanism is the October 14 rule itself, which shortens the contest window for refugees facing termination notices.
  • The plaintiffs: the documented mechanism is the lawsuit filed October 26, which pairs the rule challenge with a challenge to the broader refugee review plan.
  • Structural note: the order is temporary and preserves the status quo; it does not decide whether the rule is lawful.

Sources:

  • Reuters, October 27 — order, November 6 hold, 186,000 estimate, statements from both sides

6. States sue Treasury over the rollback of corporate ownership reporting rules

What happened: On October 27, states and the District of Columbia sued the Trump administration over its rollback of reporting requirements established under the 2021 Corporate Transparency Act, a law aimed at combating illicit finance. The administration finalized a rule in August exempting U.S. companies and individuals from reporting beneficial ownership information to the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. The lawsuit, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Oregon against Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, the Treasury Department, and the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, asks a federal court to overturn the rule. The suit argues that the executive branch cannot repeal a law by rulemaking, and says the administration effectively gutted the 2021 law by exempting more than 99.9 percent of companies originally required to disclose information about their owners. The lawsuit also lists the heads of the Justice and Homeland Security departments as defendants. Bessent cast the rule when it was made in August as "eliminating a burdensome reporting requirement for millions of law-abiding business owners without compromising our national security." The Treasury Department did not respond to a request for comment. Bloomberg Law reports the states argue the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network exceeded its authority under the Administrative Procedure Act when it finalized the August regulations.

Incentives:

  • The suing states: the documented mechanism is a federal lawsuit asking a court to overturn the August rule, which would restore the reporting requirement if granted.
  • The Treasury Department: the documented mechanism is the August rule itself, which removes the filing obligation for U.S. companies and individuals.
  • Companies and owners: the reporting requirement applies to beneficial ownership information, so the exemption removes a disclosure filing for the exempted group.
  • Structural note: outlets differ on the size of the coalition — Reuters reports 21 states and the District of Columbia, while Bloomberg Law reports 20 states and the District of Columbia — so this edition does not state a single count.

Sources:

  • Reuters, October 27 — filing, Oregon court, 99.9 percent claim, Bessent statement
  • Bloomberg Law, October 27 — Administrative Procedure Act argument, August regulations
