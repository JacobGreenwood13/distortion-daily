The Now

The week in American politics — what happened, what each side claims, and the incentive read underneath it. Dated every edition, refreshed weekly, never quietly rewritten.

By Jacob · Editor · Updated September 29, 2026 · covering Sept 28–Oct 4.

How to read this page

This page is a briefing. Every item gets the same three cuts: what happened (the verifiable part), what each side claims (the spin), and the incentive read (who gains from you believing which version). Figures come from the outlets cited in each item. Edition № 003 carries no new corrections — the № 002 correction to № 001 stands loud on the record. Old editions get archived, not memory-holed.

1. The press ban goes back to court

What happened: Late Monday, September 28, CNN, MS NOW, and Politico asked a federal judge for a preliminary injunction blocking Trump's ban on their White House access until there's a final ruling. This follows Judge Timothy Kelly's 14-day temporary restraining order, which found the bans likely violated the outlets' rights to fair legal treatment. Meanwhile the Justice Department's defense has shifted: it now says Trump lawfully banned the outlets after determining their coverage threatened national security by revealing classified information. Trump himself said on September 18 he was banning them for "reporting FAKE NEWS" and that they "shouldn't be able to constantly write or report FICTION and LIES." (Reporting: Reuters, Sept 29.)

What each side claims: The outlets: viewpoint discrimination, a First Amendment violation. The White House and DOJ: the bans protect national security. Trump personally: the coverage was fiction and lies.

The incentive read: Watch the justification move. "FAKE NEWS" is a viewpoint complaint — exactly what the first judge found likely unconstitutional. "Classified leaks" is a security complaint, which is a much stronger legal shield. The question that decides this case is the same one that should decide your trust: if the leaks are real, show the leaks. If the evidence never arrives, the justification moved because the first one was losing. Courts read the paper trail — and so should you.

2. The $810 million pocket rescission

What happened: On September 28, the White House announced it is withholding $810 million in funds Congress already appropriated — $567 million of it from programs serving refugees, asylees, and other non-citizens, per the White House's own memo. The move is a "pocket rescission": canceling funds days before the September 30 fiscal-year end, leaving Congress no time to weigh in or reallocate the money. The Government Accountability Office says the move is illegal. And the sharpest rebuke came from a Republican: Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins said OMB "does not get to decide which programs are worth funding" and called it an attempt to undermine Congress's constitutional power of the purse. (Reporting: WAMC, Sept 28, citing the White House memo, GAO, and Collins's statement.)

What each side claims: The White House: it's cutting spending Congress shouldn't have ordered. Collins and Democrats: Article I gives the spending power to Congress, not the OMB director — the separation of powers is the whole point.

The incentive read: Second year in a row — this isn't an incident, it's a strategy. And the loudest voice against it is a Republican committee chair, which tells you the real divide here runs legislature vs. executive, not red vs. blue. The timing is the entire mechanism: a rescission request with 45 days' notice is a negotiation; the same request with two days' notice is a run-out-the-clock. When the deadline does the voting, Congress never gets a say. That's not budgeting — it's confiscation with a calendar.

3. The Iran file: war powers voted down, troops leaving, sanctions on the table

What happened: The Senate voted down the Iran war powers resolution on September 25 — even with four Republican defectors, it wasn't enough. Meanwhile U.S. forces are set to pull out of their last bases in Iraq by September 30 under a withdrawal agreement struck in 2024, being carried out under Trump even as the war in Iran continues. Separately, Axios reported U.S. officials saying Trump is willing to offer Iran sanctions relief and release frozen Iranian funds in exchange for concrete nuclear-program steps; Trump called those reports "untrue" on social media. (Reporting: The Hill, AP, and Fox News on the vote; Reuters on the pullout; Axios on the sanctions offer.)

What each side claims: War-powers supporters: Congress must authorize a war it's paying for. The administration: the president already has the authority he needs. Tehran, via an IRGC spokesman: called Trump a "big liar" and accused him of concealing the truth about Iran's nuclear program.

The incentive read: The branch of government with the constitutional power to declare war just voted, again, not to exercise it — while funding every bomb. That's the whole incentive story in one vote: the authority to start a war is worth more to Congress unspent than spent, because spending it means owning the outcome. And the Iraq pullout is two-year-old momentum from a Biden-era deal, not a new dove policy — nobody in Washington gets to claim it as their peace moment. Watch the frozen funds: money moves where votes won't.

4. China: the truce got extended, the clock didn't stop

What happened: Xi Jinping arrived for the summit, and the U.S.–China tariff truce was extended to January 10. At the same time, the Graham Act's 30-day implementation deadline keeps ticking — the law requires the tariff and sanctions measures within 30 days of its September 18 signing, so by October 18, unless the White House issues a national-interest waiver. The waiver power is broad, and India is expected to seek one; China and India remain the obvious targets for the up-to-100% tariffs. (Reporting: The Hill and AP on the summit and truce; Mondaq and Akin Gump on the deadline and waiver.)

What each side claims: Hawks want the tariffs imposed on day 30. Business lobbyists want the waivers. Beijing called the law "unfriendly."

The incentive read: The truce extends past the midterms; the tariff sword lands after them. That is not a coincidence — it is a calendar. Nobody wants the price shock before the vote, and neither side of the Pacific wants the other's voters seeing what 100% tariffs do to a grocery bill. October 18 is the real story: a legal mandate, a five-weeks-out election, and a waiver pen sitting in the White House. The threat makes the headlines. The waiver makes the policy.

5. The election gets litigated before it happens

What happened: Democratic leaders sued to block federal agents from being stationed at polling places in November. (Reporting: The Guardian, The New York Times, and The Washington Post, Sept 25.) We're five weeks from the November 3 midterms, and both parties are now fighting in court over the rules of the election itself.

What each side claims: Democrats: federal agents at polls are voter intimidation. The administration: it's election security.

The incentive read: This is the election being litigated before it happens — both parties fighting over the rules of the game while claiming to protect the game. The question underneath the lawsuit is a measurable one: what does a polling place with federal agents do to turnout? And who benefits if the answer is "fewer people vote"? Whoever answers that question with data instead of slogans is the one worth listening to. Follow the turnout, not the press releases.

6. The federally funded ad

What happened: A federally funded pro-Trump television advertisement began airing weeks before the midterms. (Reporting: The Guardian and The Washington Post, Sept 25.)

What each side claims: The administration: official communication of administration achievements. Critics: taxpayer-funded campaigning, five weeks before the vote.

The incentive read: Your taxes. His message. Five weeks before Election Day. Government communications are legal; campaign ads are regulated. The line between them is where the lawyers live — and where the incentives get interesting, because the same dollar can't be both an official announcement and a campaign ad. Watch whether anyone enforces the line. Enforcement is the whole difference between a law and a suggestion.

7. Strong data, brutal rates

What happened: The 10-year Treasury climbed above 5.2% — its highest since 2007 — and mortgage rates moved back above 7% (Rodeo Realty, Sept 26). The same week delivered punishingly good economic news: weekly unemployment claims fell to 197,000, August retail sales rose 1.2% from July (up 6% from a year ago), and U.S. business activity expanded at its fastest pace in more than five years. Stocks rose anyway — investors read the rate spike as growth, not just inflation. The August PCE report, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, lands Wednesday, September 30; the September jobs report follows Friday, October 2. (Reporting: Rodeo Realty, Sept 26; MortgageDaily, Sept 27.)

What each side claims: Growth bulls: a strong economy absorbing higher rates is a soft landing. Housing bears: the Fed is strangling the housing market to save the inflation target.

The incentive read: The paradox is the story: an economy strong enough to justify more hikes is an economy whose borrowing costs keep rising — Wall Street cheers the growth while Main Street pays the mortgage. That's the distribution the Fed's forecasts never price in. Tomorrow's PCE and Friday's jobs prints decide whether October brings relief or the second hike. The numbers are the only honest press release in Washington. Wallet translation on The Ledger.

The week in one line

The press went back to court, the White House reached into Congress's wallet, the Senate declined the war-powers fight again, the tariff sword got a new sheath date, and the economy stayed strong enough to keep hurting. Follow the incentives — the performances won't tell you anything.

Held back this week: Yemen casualty figures (WHO, via a single NYT report — watching for corroboration), the Senate's TRIA reauthorization vote (real, but niche — nobody's wallet is in it this week), and the FDA nominee's abortion-pill questioning (single-source).
