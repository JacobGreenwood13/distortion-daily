1. Both uncalled Senate races widen for the Democrats as ballot deadlines pass

What happened: In Alaska, the Division of Elections added about 24,600 ballots on Tuesday, November 10, and Democrat Mary Peltola's lead over Republican Senator Dan Sullivan widened to 4,250 first-choice votes, 118,926 to about 114,676, or 48.8 percent to 47.0 percent. The Associated Press estimated about 84 percent of the vote had been counted. About 40,000 accepted ballots were still not in the results: about 32,000 absentee ballots, 5,400 questioned ballots, and 2,600 early votes. The next count update is expected Friday, November 13. Neither candidate holds a majority of first choices, so the race goes to ranked-choice tabulation on November 18 if neither tops 50 percent, and the state aims to certify November 25. The namesake candidate Dan J. Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg, holds 4,027 votes, fewer than the margin between the two leaders. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,851 votes on Tuesday night, with 953,547 votes counted and about 97 percent of the expected vote tallied, according to the Associated Press count carried by CNBC. The deadlines passed Tuesday at 5 p.m.: mail ballots postmarked by Election Day, November 3, had to reach county clerks, and voters who cast affidavit ballots without an accepted photo ID had to show one. The secretary of state's office said counties had processed 15,547 affidavit ballots as of Monday, with 76 percent accepted, and more than 18,000 by Tuesday evening. Counties must certify by Friday, November 13, and the secretary of state declares the final result December 3. Mississippi has no recount law. Hyde-Smith's lawyers have written to state officials demanding that every affidavit ballot be set apart and preserved. The Associated Press has called neither race. The Senate stands at 54 Democrats to 44 Republicans with the two races uncalled.

Incentives:

  • The Alaska count: the documented mechanism is ranked-choice tabulation, in which no candidate with a majority triggers a second round counting voters' next preferences, scheduled for November 18.
  • The Mississippi count: the documented mechanism is the affidavit-ballot process, in which counties review ballots cast by voters whose names were not on the poll book or who lacked an accepted photo ID, with about 76 percent accepted so far.
  • The legal posture: the documented mechanism is preservation, in which Hyde-Smith's lawyers asked state officials to set apart and preserve every affidavit ballot, the step that precedes a court challenge in a state with no recount law.
  • Structural note: race calls are projections based on counted votes and remaining ballots, not certifications; neither race has been called by the Associated Press.

2. Abu Dhabi talks end with a prisoner exchange agreed in principle and no breakthrough on a settlement

What happened: Two days of United States-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi ended Wednesday, November 11, with agreement in principle on a large exchange of prisoners of war and no announced breakthrough on a ceasefire or on territory. The talks, the first between the delegations since February, opened Tuesday, November 10, with a four-hour session among the envoys and continued Wednesday for under two hours. Vladimir Medinsky, head of the Russian delegation, and Rustem Umerov, who led Kyiv's delegation, both confirmed the planned exchange; Medinsky described the agreement as in principle, and Russia's state-run news agency TASS reported the exchange should happen in two to three weeks. United States envoy Jared Kushner said the final numbers would be settled Thursday, November 12. Kushner said he and United States Special Envoy Steve Witkoff will travel to Moscow on Thursday to meet President Vladimir Putin, then fly to Dublin to meet Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. On the question of European participation, Medinsky said Moscow was not opposed to participation from the European Union, and Kushner said European countries must be at the table. Ukrainian negotiator David Arakhamia said the talks had been tough, with Russia's delegation arriving with strict directives, but provided good momentum toward a solution before the end of the year. Ukraine has rejected Russia's demand to surrender the whole of the eastern Donbas region in return for peace.

Incentives:

  • The prisoner exchange: the documented mechanism is the humanitarian track, in which the two sides agreed in principle on a large swap while the territorial questions stayed unresolved, with numbers due to be finalized November 12.
  • The envoys' next step: the documented mechanism is shuttle diplomacy, in which Kushner and Witkoff carry what was discussed to Putin in Moscow on Thursday and then to Zelenskyy in Dublin.
  • The format question: the documented mechanism is the widening of the table, in which the United States mediators are pressing to bring European partners into talks Moscow had kept trilateral, and Medinsky said Moscow was not opposed to European Union participation.
  • Structural note: "in principle" means the exchange is agreed but not complete; neither the exact number of prisoners nor the timing has been disclosed.

3. Israel's new Knesset is sworn in with no government, and Eisenkot's deadline runs to December 6

What happened: Israel's 26th Knesset was sworn in on Tuesday, November 10, after the October 27 election left neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu nor Gadi Eisenkot with a parliamentary majority. President Isaac Herzog, presiding over the ceremony, told lawmakers they must do everything to avoid a repeat election. Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, holds President Herzog's mandate to form a government since November 8 and has until December 6, with a possible 14-day extension to December 20. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister. Netanyahu told the new parliament it should establish a national unity government and warned that Iran is working to revive the nuclear threat; Eisenkot rejected the offer the same afternoon and said the country must act urgently to establish a national emergency government. Moments after the opening of parliament, Eisenkot and his allies submitted bills to establish a state commission of inquiry into the October 7, 2023 attacks, to impose term limits on the prime minister, and to repeal parts of the judicial overhaul passed by the previous Knesset. The bloc also moved to replace Knesset Speaker Amir Ohana, with a vote scheduled for Tuesday, November 17, and Matan Kahana of Yashar expected to win after 67 lawmakers signed the request to remove Ohana. The seat counts are Netanyahu's bloc with 53 of 120 seats, Eisenkot's bloc with 48, and the Arab parties with 19; a government needs 61. Eisenkot's bloc agreed to put Avigdor Lieberman, leader of Yisrael Beiteinu, in charge of the Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee.

Incentives:

  • The mandate: the documented mechanism is the 28-day formation clock under Basic Law: The Government, in which Eisenkot has until December 6, with a possible 14-day presidential extension to December 20.
  • The speaker vote: the documented mechanism is control of the legislative agenda, in which replacing the speaker lets Eisenkot's bloc advance bills while coalition talks continue, with the vote set for November 17.
  • The coalition arithmetic: the documented mechanism is the 61-seat threshold, in which Eisenkot's 48 seats need ultra-Orthodox backing, Arab-party support for a minority government, or a unity government with Netanyahu.
  • Structural note: receiving the mandate is an opportunity to negotiate a government, not an appointment as prime minister; no coalition agreement has been announced.

  • Le Monde, November 11 — speaker vote November 17, Kahana expected, inquiry commission and judicial overhaul repeal bills, negotiations with ultra-Orthodox lists
  • Silent Dossier, November 10 — Netanyahu unity-government proposal, Eisenkot rejection, 53-48-19 seat split, 67 signatures to remove Ohana, final count November 4

4. Iran's proposal reaches Washington as Tehran rules out sending enriched uranium abroad

What happened: Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met Qatari mediators in New York on Monday, November 9, to discuss a proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The plan, announced Friday at the United Nations General Assembly, calls on the United States to release frozen Iranian funds, lift sanctions, and end its naval blockade of Iranian ports; Araghchi said those steps could be completed within four or five days, allowing the strait to reopen and negotiations on a final agreement, including on Iran's nuclear program, to begin by the seventh day. The proposal reached the White House on Monday, carried by Qatar's prime minister, and included the release of $8 billion of Iranian money frozen in Qatar. President Donald Trump rejected the proposal on Saturday, calling it unacceptable. A United States official said the talks were positive and constructive but there would be no agreement unless Iran's nuclear program is addressed, and that Trump is willing to relieve sanctions and release frozen funds in exchange for progress on nuclear issues. On Wednesday, November 11, Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsen Rezaei said Iran would not allow enriched nuclear material to leave the country, and said no negotiations were currently taking place between Tehran and Washington. Before United States and Israeli strikes against three major Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025, the International Atomic Energy Agency estimated Iran held 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent. Iran closed the strait shortly after the United States and Israel launched their offensive on February 28. The strait handled roughly 20 percent of the world's oil and gas supplies before the war. Attacks on vessels continued: 37 ships were attacked in October, the highest monthly total since the conflict began, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy. Brent crude settled at $91.49 a barrel on Tuesday after trading below $90 for the first time since August, then rose about 1.4 percent on Wednesday to $92.77, with United States West Texas Intermediate at $86.80.

Incentives:

  • Tehran: the documented mechanism is the strait itself, in which Iran ties reopening the waterway to the release of frozen funds, an end to sanctions, and an end to the port blockade.
  • Washington: the documented mechanism is the nuclear condition, in which the United States says no agreement to end the war comes without addressing Iran's nuclear program, while offering sanctions relief and released funds for progress on it.
  • The oil market: the documented mechanism is the price response, in which Brent fell below $90 on Tuesday as traders priced in a possible deal, then rose Wednesday after Rezaei ruled out transferring enriched material.
  • Structural note: Rezaei's statement that no negotiations are taking place conflicts with the Qatari channel described by Araghchi and the United States official; the two accounts describe the same week from opposite sides.

Sources:

  • Reuters via Investors Hub, November 11 — Brent $92.77 and WTI $86.80 Wednesday, Rezaei on enriched material, 37 ships attacked in October, LSEG on mine clearance and insurance
  • Demokratis, November 11 — Rezaei reaffirms no uranium transfer, IAEA estimate of 440.9 kilograms at 60 percent, no negotiations currently taking place

5. October inflation comes in above forecast with no annual rate, and markets fall as rate-hike bets rise

What happened: The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Tuesday, November 10, that the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers rose 0.4 percent in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, after rising 0.6 percent in September, and that core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3 percent for a third straight month. Both readings came in a tenth of a point above economists' forecasts of 0.3 percent and 0.2 percent. Shelter rose 0.3 percent and accounted for over 30 percent of the monthly increase; food rose 0.5 percent and energy rose 0.7 percent, after a 5.2 percent rise in September; used cars and trucks fell 1.2 percent. The Bureau published no 12-month inflation rate: it did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of the lapse in appropriations during the 43-day shutdown that ran October 1 to November 12, 2025, so it could not publish October 2025 indexes for most series. The last published annual reading is September's, at 3.7 percent headline and 2.6 percent core. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said the morning of the release that a 50-basis-point increase should be considered at the December meeting if core inflation came in around 0.3 percent or higher. Markets moved on the print: the S&P 500 fell 0.38 percent to 7,520.93, the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.74 percent to 25,065.88, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 0.30 percent to 52,231.89. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.983 percent after touching 5.03 percent during the session, and the two-year yield jumped 19.1 basis points to 4.738 percent. Traders fully priced in an increase of at least 25 basis points at the Federal Reserve's December 8 and 9 meeting. Nvidia fell 5.47 percent to $168.89 as its board investigates chief executive Jensen Huang. The next Consumer Price Index release, covering November, is scheduled for December 10, the day after the Federal Reserve meeting ends.

Incentives:

  • The data: the documented mechanism is the missing base month, in which the 2025 shutdown stopped October 2025 price collection, so no official year-over-year rate exists for October 2026 and the monthly readings carry the full weight of the report.
  • The rate decision: the documented mechanism is the December meeting, in which the October report is the last inflation data the Federal Open Market Committee will see before it meets December 8 and 9, since the November report arrives December 10.
  • The bond market: the documented mechanism is the yield move, in which the 10-year yield rose to 4.983 percent and the two-year yield jumped 19.1 basis points as traders raised the odds of a December increase.
  • Structural note: any October year-over-year inflation rate quoted anywhere is not a Bureau of Labor Statistics figure; the Bureau published none.

6. Congress returns with 16 House session days before federal funding runs out December 11

What happened: Congress returned to Washington on Monday, November 9, with federal agencies funded only through December 11, 2026. The stopgap law, the Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 (H.R. 6500), became law September 2 as Public Law 119-103 and funds agencies at fiscal 2026 levels until December 11 or until full-year spending bills replace it. The House agreed to the Senate's version on September 1 by a vote of 370 to 48. The House calendar published by Majority Leader Steve Scalise shows 16 days in session between Monday and the deadline: November 9 through 12, November 17 through 20, November 30 through December 3, and December 8 through 11, with four more session days, December 14 through 17, after funding is due to lapse. To avoid a lapse, both chambers must pass either full-year appropriations or another stopgap, and the president must sign it, by December 11. The same law extends federal highway and transit programs to December 11, with Highway Trust Fund spending authority running to December 12. The law also blocks the Office of Management and Budget from finalizing its proposed overhaul of federal grant rules through December 11, and requires the office to send the appropriations committees a list of rescissions by November 20. None of the 12 annual appropriations bills for fiscal 2027 has been completed. Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate on November 3, and the new Congress is sworn in January 3, 2027.

Incentives:

  • The calendar: the documented mechanism is the session count, in which 16 House session days remain before the deadline and four more fall after funding is due to lapse.
  • The stopgap: the documented mechanism is the continuing resolution, in which agencies run at fiscal 2026 levels and cannot start projects that lacked funding authority in fiscal 2026.
  • The lame duck: the documented mechanism is the turnover, in which the outgoing majorities hold the pen on funding through December 11 while the incoming majorities take office January 3, 2027.
  • Structural note: the December 11 deadline is a funding lapse, not a shutdown that has already happened; the government is funded through that date under Public Law 119-103.
