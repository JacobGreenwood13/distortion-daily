ARPANET, The Cold War, and The Red Scare's Long Shadow

October 29, 1969. A graduate student at UCLA sits down at a terminal and tries to log in to a computer at Stanford, hundreds of miles away. He types L. He types O. The system crashes.

By Jacob · Editor · November 9, 2026 · 16 min read

BORN INSIDE A SURVEILLANCE STATE

October 29, 1969. A graduate student at UCLA sits down at a terminal and tries to log in to a computer at Stanford, hundreds of miles away. He types L. He types O. The system crashes. The first message ever sent across ARPANET — the network that becomes the internet — is "LO."

That same month, the FBI is running COINTELPRO at full strength: illegal operations against civil rights leaders, antiwar groups, and the Black Panther Party. The NSA is collecting around 150,000 international telegrams a month through Project SHAMROCK, handed over by Western Union, RCA, and ITT, no warrants involved. Project MINARET keeps a watch list of roughly 1,650 American citizens — Martin Luther King Jr., Muhammad Ali, journalists, even sitting Senators.

The internet was born inside a surveillance state. Not near one. Inside one. Pay close attention to that, because everything in this story follows from it.

HOW IT ACTUALLY WORKED

The core idea came from Paul Baran, working at RAND for the Air Force in the early 1960s. Baran's design broke messages into small blocks, each one finding its own route through the network, reassembling at the destination. No central control point. A central point would be the first thing destroyed in an attack, so he designed around it. Donald Davies in Britain arrived at the same concept independently and gave it the name that stuck: packet switching.

Larry Roberts, ARPANET's program manager, picked up packet switching from Davies' team at a 1967 symposium. Wesley Clark proposed dedicated minicomputers — Interface Message Processors, IMPs — to handle the network traffic instead of wiring host computers directly together. BBN built them. The first four nodes went live in late 1969: UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, Utah.

Routing was distributed and adaptive from the start. The builders' own completion report lists the options they weighed — fixed, random, centrally controlled, distributed adaptive — and says they chose distributed adaptive. Centralized control of the data was on the table. They rejected it.

Now the rumor you've probably heard: ARPANET was built so America could keep communicating after a nuclear war. The Internet Society calls that story a false rumor, directly. The funded mission was cheaper and more boring: a handful of powerful computers existed, they cost a fortune, and researchers at different universities all needed time on them. Share the machines instead of buying more machines. The fear of a decapitation strike was real — it drove real programs like the Looking Glass airborne command post — but it was the climate ARPANET grew up in, not its job description.

THE CONTROL POINTS CAME IN THROUGH THE BACK DOOR

The data plane was distributed. The management plane was not.

BBN ran a Network Control Center that watched the health of every IMP on the network, collected traffic statistics, and could push new software to every node remotely. One person could reload the entire network in an hour or two. The alternative was mailing paper tapes to every site and coordinating a nationwide team for a month. The NCC's own design paper states plainly that the network would survive its failure. Central oversight was a convenience, and they built it anyway.

Naming was centralized too. Every host name on the network lived in a single file, HOSTS.TXT, maintained at SRI. The 1982 RFC documenting this called it "an interim solution on the way to a decentralized, distributed name/address translation service." That service became DNS.

Then the institutional centralization: one funder (the Defense Department), one IMP vendor (BBN), one long-haul carrier (AT&T). In July 1975, operational control moved from DARPA to the Defense Communications Agency — the military's own network bureaucracy. In 1983, the network was administratively split into ARPANET and MILNET, the military half.

The technology worked fine distributed — the builders proved it. Centralization entered as the practical price of running the thing: monitoring, software updates, naming, money, wires. Every one of those central points sat under a government that was, at that exact moment, intercepting telegrams by the truckload. The overreach was structurally invited by who held the levers. That distinction matters, and we will come back to it.

THE WAR WAS IN THE ROOM

ARPANET's universities were battlegrounds. In March 1969, MIT held a one-day research stoppage against war research. In April 1969, over a thousand Stanford protesters occupied the Applied Electronics Laboratory for nine days; the trustees responded by spinning off SRI so its war research could continue at arm's length. The Mansfield Amendment of 1969 demanded that Defense-funded research show a direct military relationship — a direct hit on ARPA's model of funding open-ended basic research.

The moral dilemma of the era is on the congressional record, in the participants' own words. Paul Baran himself testified before Congress in July 1966 about a White House plan for a National Data Center — a comprehensive file on every American. Baran warned that interconnected computer systems "can pose the danger of loss of the individual's right to privacy — as we know it today." In the same hearings, one congressman said "the computer neither forgives nor forgets." Another said the problem was "akin to the changes wrought in our national life with the dawning of the nuclear age."

A decade later, Senator Frank Church said it on national television: the government had "perfected a technological capability that enables us to monitor the messages that go through the air... that capability at any time could be turned around on the American people, and no American would have any privacy left... I know the capacity that is there to make tyranny total in America."

What you will not find is the builders of ARPANET wrestling with any of this in their own papers. Search their reports for anguish about military money and the page is blank. The dilemma lived in Congress and in the streets. The engineers built.

THE RED SCARE PLAYBOOK

To understand what "Russian interference" means today, start with the original machinery of fear.

Truman's 1947 loyalty program ran millions of federal employees through loyalty forms and FBI name checks. The FBI's secret "Responsibilities Program" circulated anonymous, unreliable allegations of communist affiliation to governors and civic leaders from 1951 to 1955. Targets lost their jobs with no process, condemned by anonymous allegations they never saw. The McCarran Act of 1950 authorized detaining citizens during "internal security emergencies." A 1954 law, the Communist Control Act — "An Act to outlaw the Communist Party" — stripped the Party of its legal rights and subjected knowing members to Internal Security Act penalties.

The mechanism: secret lists, unreliable vetting, accusations that can't be answered, and a foreign threat big enough to justify all of it. Remember that mechanism. It comes back.

WHAT RUSSIA ACTUALLY DID — THE DOCUMENTED RECORD

Keep this section fenced off from the next one. The fence is the whole point.

Soviet active measures are documented history: the KGB pushed the claim that AIDS was created at Fort Detrick. Bogus KKK letters were forged to inflame American racial tension.

For 2016, the primary documents establish the following. The January 2017 Intelligence Community Assessment says Putin ordered an influence campaign to undermine faith in the democratic process and denigrate Clinton, with a preference for Trump. The same assessment explicitly declined to judge whether Russian activities changed the outcome: "We did not make an assessment of the impact that Russian activities had on the outcome of the 2016 election."

The bipartisan Senate Intelligence Committee, across five volumes, found Russia engaged in an "aggressive, multi-faceted effort" to influence the outcome, confirmed Putin ordered the DNC hack-and-leak, and called the Trump campaign chairman's sharing of polling data with a Russian intelligence officer a "grave counterintelligence threat." The Mueller Report found interference "in sweeping and systematic fashion" through the Internet Research Agency's social-media campaign and GRU hacking released through cutouts, indicted 12 GRU officers, and did not establish that the Trump campaign conspired with the Russian government.

On scale: the IRA spent roughly $100,000 on Facebook and Instagram ads over two years, but the Senate Committee stressed paid ads were not the core of the operation. The organic content was the weapon: 61,500+ Facebook posts, 116,000 Instagram posts, 10.4 million tweets. Facebook estimated up to 126 million people may have been served IRA content — an upper-bound estimate of exposure, not persuasion.

Beyond 2016: in 2019 a Montenegrin court convicted 13 people, including two alleged Russian military intelligence officers tried in absentia, of plotting a coup during the 2016 parliamentary elections — then the verdict was annulled on appeal in 2021, and a retrial acquitted all 13 in 2024. France's 2017 "Macron leaks" went unattributed at the time — France's cyber chief said the attack was so generic it could have been anyone — but France's Foreign Ministry formally accused the GRU in April 2025.

None of that is in dispute between the parties that investigated it. A Republican-led Senate committee and a Republican-appointed special counsel both validated the core findings.

WHAT GOT INFLATED OR WEAPONIZED — KEPT SEPARATE

Hamilton 68. The Alliance for Securing Democracy's dashboard tracked around 600 accounts it claimed were linked to Russian influence, using a secret list and undisclosed methodology. Twitter reverse-engineered the list: of the accounts registered in Russia, most belonged to RT employees; the rest were largely ordinary American, British, and Canadian users. Twitter's trust-and-safety chief internally called it "bullshit," writing that it "falsely accuses a bunch of legitimate right-leaning accounts of being Russian bots." ASD's own fact sheet concedes it "did not individually review or verify all accounts."

The Vermont-grid retraction. In December 2016 the Washington Post reported Russian hackers had penetrated the U.S. electricity grid. The editor's note now reads: the hacked computer at Burlington Electric "was not attached to the grid." DHS confirmed the grid was not penetrated.

The Alfa Bank server. Slate reported a secret Trump–Alfa Bank communications channel in October 2016. The FBI's contemporaneous position, reported by the New York Times, was that there could be an innocuous explanation like marketing email or spam.

The Steele dossier and the FISA record. The Clinton campaign and DNC paid for the dossier through Perkins Coie and Fusion GPS; the FEC later fined both for misreporting the payments as "legal services" instead of opposition research. The DOJ Inspector General's 2019 report found the Crossfire Hurricane investigation had an authorized purpose and predication — the dossier was not the basis for opening it — but the FBI made 17 significant errors and omissions in the Carter Page FISA applications, kept surveilling despite mounting evidence the Steele material was unreliable, and never told the court Page had been an operational contact of another U.S. agency.

The 51-officials letter. In October 2020, 51 former intelligence officials signed a letter saying the Hunter Biden laptop story had "all the classic earmarks of a Russian information operation" while conceding they had no direct evidence. Twitter locked the Post's account and Facebook throttled the story. By 2022, major outlets had authenticated emails from the laptop via cryptographic signatures. In 2023 testimony, the letter's organizer said Biden-campaign outreach had "triggered" the letter and that he acted because he wanted Biden to win. No U.S. intelligence assessment has ever substantiated Russian involvement in the laptop's appearance.

Read the two sections together and the pattern is plain: genuine operations exist, and genuine operations get used as cover for claims that exceed the evidence. The scare works because the seed exists.

OUR READ

The following is analysis, not established fact.

On the scare tactic: "Russian interference" gets deployed as an election scare tactic wherever specific claims outrun the evidence — Hamilton 68, the grid story, the 51-officials letter. The framing collapses wherever the operations were genuinely serious: the GRU hack-and-leak, the bipartisan validation. The honest version distinguishes the documented operations from the weaponized framing instead of treating all interference talk as fabrication.

On the overreach thesis: the evidence supports "structurally invited," not "inevitable." Central management points under a surveilling government created the temptation and the capability; the builders' own documents show the central points were conveniences, and some were later decentralized. Our read is that a government already running COINTELPRO does not leave centralized levers sitting unused forever — but the record proves the invitation, not the certainty.

On the Red Scare's afterlife: the machinery never retired, it changed targets. Secret lists of Americans circulated as suspected foreign agents, unreliable vetting, foreign-threat framing turned against domestic figures — Hamilton 68 rhymes with the FBI's 1950s Responsibilities Program — secret lists, allegations the targets never saw. The discontinuity deserves equal weight: the Soviet Union was an expansionist superpower selling world revolution; modern Russia is a nationalist petro-state pursuing regional influence. Anti-communism accused Americans of believing an ideology; interference framing targets contact and amplification — a wider net. The rhyme is in the methods, not the enemy.

THE NAVY BUILT THE DARK WEB'S FRONT DOOR

Definitions first, because the terms get abused. The deep web is everything search engines don't index: your email, your bank account, your medical portal, paywalled academic databases. Ordinary infrastructure, mostly. The dark web is a small corner inside it: sites that only load through anonymity software like Tor.

Tor was built by the U.S. Navy. In the mid-1990s, researchers at the Naval Research Laboratory developed onion routing with DARPA and Office of Naval Research funding, to protect American intelligence communications from traffic analysis. The key design insight: an anonymity network used only by spies identifies its users instantly. The tool needed a crowd — ordinary people, journalists, activists — to hide the agents in. So it had to be released publicly. Tor's alpha launched in 2002 with its code released under a free and open software license, and the Tor Project became a nonprofit in 2006. For years it was majority U.S.-government funded.

Sit with that irony a moment. The same government that built the surveillance machinery built the anonymity machinery, because its own spies needed cover. Then criminals moved into the crowd, and the government started warning you about the neighborhood it built.

WHAT MOVED INTO THE CROWD

The documented criminal record is ugly. Silk Road ran from 2011 to 2013: over 100,000 buyers, $200M+ in drug sales per prosecution figures. Ross Ulbricht got life in 2015 and a pardon in January 2025. The case's own investigators were dirty — a DEA agent got 78 months for stealing bitcoin and extorting Ulbricht, a Secret Service agent got 71 months. AlphaBay fell in July 2017 to an FBI/DEA-led operation; its alleged administrator was found dead in a Thai jail cell a week after arrest. Dutch police seized the Hansa market and ran it covertly for a month, logging plaintext passwords and copying encrypted orders, collecting around 10,000 foreign buyer addresses. In the Playpen case, the FBI seized a child-exploitation site and operated it for 13 days, deploying malware that reported users' real IP addresses — hacking over 8,000 computers in 120 countries on a single Virginia magistrate's warrant. The 2019 Welcome to Video takedown used bitcoin tracing to arrest 337 users across 11 countries and rescue 23 child victims.

The documented legitimate record exists alongside it. SecureDrop, the whistleblower-submission system running on Tor, is used by the Guardian, the New York Times, and the Washington Post. Egyptian Tor usage roughly quadrupled in the days before the January 2011 protests, then dropped to zero during Mubarak's full internet shutdown. U.S. internet-freedom funding supported circumvention tools reaching 40 million-plus monthly users in China, Iran, and Russia.

The tool is dual-use. That is the documented pattern: a government anonymity project became public infrastructure used by everyone, including criminals. Criminal use was not the designers' intent. The crowd was the point, and the crowd contains multitudes.

THE PATTERN TODAY

The internet's backbone is concentrated: roughly 13 to 15 Tier-1 networks carry the whole thing, and a handful of companies dominate DNS and cloud. The management points got bigger, not smaller.

Section 702 surveillance, the modern descendant of the programs Church investigated, runs under authorities created in response to the first era's abuses — FISA in 1978, amended in 2008. The Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board's 2023 report found the FBI ran over 200,000 U.S.-person queries in 2022 and nearly five million from 2019 to 2022 "with little justification." The Board called the program "highly valuable" but creating "serious privacy and civil liberties risks," and the government could not say how many Americans' communications were incidentally collected. In 2017 the NSA ended "abouts" collection after a FISA court judge called noncompliance "a very serious Fourth Amendment issue."

The 2013 Snowden disclosures revealed compelled production from major tech companies and backbone traffic tapping. The USA FREEDOM Act of 2015 ended the bulk telephony-metadata program. New laws, same levers.

THE LESSON

A network built to share computers grew control points because control points are convenient. The control points sat under a government already spying on its citizens. The scare tactics that justified the spying never died; they updated their vocabulary. And the anonymity tool the Navy built to protect its own agents became the thing your government warns you about.

Every era builds the machine it claims to fear. Pay close attention to who holds the levers — it's never the crowd.
