1. Colom wins Mississippi; Alaska remains uncalled with Peltola ahead by 5,800

What happened: The Associated Press called Mississippi's United States Senate race for Democrat Scott Colom on Wednesday, November 11, after counties reported updated results including affidavit ballots. Colom led Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 4,669 votes out of 962,237 counted, 0.5 percentage points. Colom is the first Black senator from Mississippi since Reconstruction — Blanche K. Bruce's term ended in 1881 — and the first Democrat elected to the Senate from the state since 1982. Hyde-Smith conceded in a video posted to social media shortly after the call. The win gives Democrats 55 seats in the new Congress, with Alaska still undecided. Counties certify by Friday, November 13, and the secretary of state declares the final result December 3. In Alaska, the Division of Elections' November 12 count showed Democrat Mary Peltola with 125,157 first-choice votes (49.08 percent) and Republican Senator Dan Sullivan with 119,357 (46.80 percent), a lead of 5,800, with about 88 percent of the vote counted according to the Associated Press. Republican Gerald Heikes holds 5,490 votes and Dan J. Sullivan Jr., a retired teacher from Petersburg, holds 4,199. Peltola's campaign declared victory on November 12; the Associated Press has not called the race. On Wednesday, Superior Court Judge Thomas Matthews denied the Peltola campaign's request for the names of voters whose absentee ballots were rejected, calling the request "not reasonable" in the middle of the count. Neither candidate holds a majority of first choices, so the race goes to ranked-choice tabulation on November 18 if neither tops 50 percent, and the state aims to certify November 25.

Incentives:

  • The Mississippi count: the documented mechanism is the affidavit-ballot process, in which counties reviewed ballots cast by voters whose names were not on the poll book or who lacked an accepted photo ID, with about 76 percent accepted.
  • The Alaska count: the documented mechanism is ranked-choice tabulation, scheduled for November 18, in which voters' next preferences are counted if no candidate holds a majority of first choices.
  • The court ruling: the documented mechanism is the rejected-ballot list, in which the judge declined to order the Division of Elections to hand over names and accept-or-reject codes for rejected absentee ballots during the count.
  • Structural note: race calls are projections based on counted votes and remaining ballots, not certifications; the Associated Press has called Mississippi and not Alaska.

2. Moscow and Dublin shuttle ends with Europe invited to the next round; Putin holds the four-region demand

What happened: United States envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Thursday, November 12, for about two and a half hours, then flew to Dublin to meet Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy the same evening. Witkoff said Putin agreed to let European representatives join the next round of talks, though Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov did not confirm the agreement. Kushner said the sides are working toward the largest prisoner exchange of the war; Zelenskyy said negotiators discussed 1,000 prisoners from each side, possibly rising to 1,500, and a Russian general said the exchange should take place within two to three weeks. No date or venue for the next round has been announced. The Institute for the Study of War assessed on November 12 that Putin still demands the four Ukrainian regions of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson, including territory the Russian army has not captured. Putin proposed a five-day humanitarian operation in Kostyantynivka; Kyiv said a similar move in Oleshky had been used to insert military assets and demanded clarification. Fighting continued on November 12, with Russia and Ukraine exchanging drone and missile attacks.

Incentives:

  • The prisoner exchange: the documented mechanism is the humanitarian track, in which the two sides agreed in principle on a large swap while the territorial questions stayed unresolved, with the final numbers still being negotiated.
  • The format: the documented mechanism is the widening of the table, in which the United States mediators are pressing to bring European partners into talks Moscow had kept trilateral, and Witkoff said Putin agreed while Ushakov did not confirm.
  • The territorial demand: the documented mechanism is the four-region claim, in which Russia's stated border under its own law includes Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson, and Ukraine has rejected surrendering the Donbas.
  • Structural note: the envoys' account and the Kremlin aide's account differ on whether European participation was agreed; no next-round date has been set.

Sources:

  • New York Post, November 12 — constructive and substantive discussion with Putin, envoys to brief Zelenskyy in Dublin, talks focused on pathways to ending the war
  • Europe Says, November 13 — Moscow meeting about two and a half hours, Dublin meeting November 12, Putin rejected foreign troops and proposed a five-day humanitarian operation in Kostyantynivka
  • The Oceania Cables, November 13 — envoys met Zelenskyy at Casement Aerodrome on the evening of November 12, exchange numbers 1,000 to 1,500 each side, no date set for the next round
  • Nikk, November 13 — Institute for the Study of War assessment: Putin still demands four regions including uncaptured territory, Europe's participation advanced, territory and security guarantees unresolved

3. Huang resigns at Nvidia; shares fall as much as 14 percent before recovering part of the loss

What happened: Nvidia announced on Thursday, November 12, that Jensen Huang has resigned as president, chief executive officer, and a member of the board, effective immediately. The board accepted the resignation on November 11, according to the company's Form 8-K filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress, who has held the finance post since September 2013, serves as interim chief executive and keeps the finance job while the board searches for a permanent successor, considering internal and external candidates. Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993 and former chief executive of Synopsys, was appointed the board's first-ever chair. Neither the filing nor the company's release gives a reason for the resignation. The resignation followed a board investigation opened November 8 into whether Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and reporting that he had an affair with Alice Huang, a woman United States officials suspect of being a Chinese intelligence asset, a claim Beijing denies and which has not been publicly confirmed. The New York Times reported November 11 that Huang had an affair with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of health care. Huang apologized to employees on November 8 for being unfaithful to his wife. President Donald Trump confirmed November 9 that the FBI is examining the allegations. A shareholder class action was filed November 9 in the Northern District of California. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott asked Huang to testify publicly on December 3 and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on December 10, warning subpoenas are possible; Huang has agreed to testify before the House Foreign Affairs Committee ahead of Thanksgiving, according to CNBC. Shares opened at $150.99 on November 12, 13.09 percent below the November 11 close of $173.73, fell as much as 14.35 percent during the session, and closed at $166.19, down 4.34 percent, erasing about $182 billion in market value in the session. The company's market value ended the day just above $4 trillion, against about $4.5 trillion at the previous Friday's close. Nvidia reports third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on Tuesday, November 17, as previously scheduled.

Incentives:

  • The filing: the documented mechanism is the 8-K, in which the company disclosed the resignation, the interim appointment, and the new board chair without stating a reason or reporting any finding from the board's investigation.
  • The market: the documented mechanism is the opening gap, in which the stock opened 13.09 percent lower, fell as much as 14.35 percent, and recovered to a 4.34 percent loss by the close, with retail buyers purchasing a net $111 million in the first 30 minutes, according to Vanda Research cited by CNBC.
  • The congressional track: the documented mechanism is the testimony request, in which the Senate Banking Committee set December 3 for Huang and December 10 for Lutnick, with subpoenas possible if they do not appear voluntarily.
  • Structural note: the espionage allegation against Alice Huang has not been publicly confirmed, and the company's filing does not link the resignation to it.

4. Four tankers hit in the Strait of Hormuz; Brent tops $95 as Trump threatens renewed strikes

What happened: Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said two supertankers were disabled by explosions on the night of Wednesday, November 11, after ignoring warnings against using what Tehran called an unauthorized route through the southern part of the strait, and were left burning with a combined cargo of more than four million barrels of oil. Iran's army later said two more tankers struck mines on Thursday afternoon, November 12, while sailing through the eastern strait after ignoring warnings and disabling their radar systems. Tehran named none of the four vessels, and Reuters said it could not immediately verify the claims. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency reported a vessel hit by an unidentified projectile about 14 nautical miles northwest of Ras Al-Khaimah on Wednesday while exiting the strait, causing a fire in its engine room; the vessel, the Dominica Prosperity, run by South Korea's Sinokor, was hit on November 11 and all 22 crew were evacuated. On Friday, November 13, the Revolutionary Guards Navy said a tanker carrying liquefied gas struck a naval mine and exploded on an unauthorized route; no vessel name, casualties, or independent confirmation were available. Brent crude settled at $95.82 a barrel on Thursday, up $3.06 or 3.3 percent, while United States West Texas Intermediate closed at $88.74, up $2.21 or 2.6 percent. President Donald Trump threatened renewed military strikes against Iran on November 12. The International Maritime Organization has verified more than 130 attacks on vessels in the strait since February 28. October saw 37 attacks, the highest monthly total since the conflict began, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy, and at least 10 ships were hit in the first 10 days of November. The International Energy Agency said on Friday that the oil market is back in surplus as Gulf exports recover.

Incentives:

  • Tehran: the documented mechanism is the strait itself, in which Iran ties passage to its warnings about routes, while its proposal to reopen the waterway in exchange for released funds, lifted sanctions, and an end to the port blockade remains rejected by Washington.
  • The oil market: the documented mechanism is the price response, in which Brent rose 3.3 percent on Thursday after the tanker claims, while the International Energy Agency said the market is back in surplus.
  • The verification gap: the documented mechanism is the unnamed vessels, in which Tehran disclosed no ship names or nationalities for the four tankers, and the only independently reported strike is the Dominica Prosperity.
  • Structural note: the Friday gas-tanker claim comes from Iranian state media via Reuters, with no confirmation from the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency or the shipowner as of Friday morning.

Sources:

  • The Curtain, November 13 — four tankers hit Wednesday and Thursday, Brent $95.82 and WTI $88.74, Dominica Prosperity hit November 11, 130 attacks verified since February 28
  • MLQ, November 13 — Friday gas-tanker claim, no vessel name or independent confirmation, Brent $96.19 and WTI $88.58 Friday morning, 37 attacks in October
  • Red Bear, November 13 — two supertankers disabled Wednesday, two more Thursday, combined cargo over four million barrels, Trump threatened renewed strikes November 12
  • FXStreet, November 13 — Energy Information Administration: crude stockpiles up 9 million barrels in the week ending November 5, four tankers hit in the strait

5. Senate Democrats tie the December 11 funding deadline to a ban on Iran-war spending

What happened: Four Senate Democrats — Michael Bennet of Colorado, Tammy Duckworth of Illinois, Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, and Bernie Sanders of Vermont, an independent who caucuses with Democrats — said in a joint statement on Thursday, November 12, that they will not accept a short-term spending patch that does not include language restricting the Trump administration from using appropriated funds to continue the war against Iran. "As we approach the December 11 funding deadline, we should not accept a short-term spending deal that continues to provide billions of additional dollars for President Trump's war of choice," the statement said. Senator Tim Kaine of Virginia told reporters on Thursday that he also would not support a short-term continuing resolution unless it blocks money for the war. Senator Patty Murray of Washington, the top Democrat on the Senate Appropriations Committee, said Democrats are discussing whether to press the December 11 deadline in the funding fight. Federal agencies are funded through December 11 under Public Law 119-103, and none of the 12 annual appropriations bills for fiscal 2027 has been enacted. The war has cost the United States military $47.1 billion as of October 16, according to figures United States Central Command provided to Congress, obtained by Reuters and USA Today: about $29 billion to replace expended munitions, $6.1 billion for lost equipment and platforms, and $11.9 billion in operating costs. The total is up from the $37.5 billion Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reported to senators in July, and it excludes the cost of repairing bases damaged by Iranian strikes. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in September that the war had cost about $38 billion through August 1 and could add $2 billion to $3 billion for each month it continues.

Incentives:

  • The condition: the documented mechanism is the December 11 deadline, in which a funding lapse begins December 12 without a new law, and a group of senators is conditioning their support for a stopgap on language restricting Iran-war spending.
  • The cost: the documented mechanism is the unfunded replacement bill, in which about $35 billion of the $47.1 billion covers munitions and equipment that must be bought again, and neither sum is covered by current funding.
  • The supplemental: the documented mechanism is the stalled request, in which the White House sent Congress an $87.6 billion supplemental request in June, about $67 billion of it for the Pentagon, and lawmakers have not approved it.
  • Structural note: the statement is a position by four senators, not a chamber vote; no stopgap text has been introduced.

Sources:

  • The Center Square, November 12 — Bennet, Duckworth, Van Hollen, and Sanders joint statement, no short-term deal without a ban on Iran-war spending, war cost $47.1 billion
  • The Hill, November 12 — Kaine will not support a continuing resolution unless it blocks war money, Murray on Democrats discussing options, funding runs out December 11
  • USA Today, October 27 — Central Command estimate $47.1 billion as of October 16, $29 billion munitions, $6.1 billion equipment, excludes base damage, 65 percent of Patriot interceptor stockpile expended
  • Reuters via BusinessWorld, October 28 — Pentagon data over $47 billion, up more than 25 percent from the July estimate of $37.5 billion, $29 billion to replace munitions

6. October producer prices rise 6.5 percent over the year, the fastest since November 2022

What happened: The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday, November 13, that the Producer Price Index for final demand rose 0.3 percent in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, after an upwardly revised 1.4 percent rise in September, and 6.5 percent over the 12 months ended in October, the largest annual increase since November 2022, when prices rose 7.4 percent. The monthly rise came in below the 0.4 percent economists surveyed by Dow Jones had expected, and the annual rate below the 6.6 percent forecast. Final demand goods rose 0.5 percent in October and 10.2 percent over the year, the largest 12-month rise since October 2022; final demand services rose 0.2 percent, advancing for a fifth straight month. The index less foods, energy, and trade services rose 0.5 percent for a second straight month and 4.9 percent over the year. Within goods, prices for fresh and dry vegetables jumped 26.7 percent in October after a 30.7 percent rise in September, and basic organic chemicals advanced 3.9 percent; diesel fuel prices fell 7.9 percent. Electronic components and accessories rose 4.2 percent in October and 31.7 percent over the year, the highest annual rate on record in Bureau data going back to December 1966. The report is the last inflation input before the Federal Reserve's December 8 and 9 meeting, where a quarter-point increase is fully priced in. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes October personal consumption expenditures inflation on November 25, and the next producer price release is scheduled for December 15.

Incentives:

  • The data: the documented mechanism is the annual rate, in which the 6.5 percent 12-month rise is the largest since November 2022, carried by a 10.2 percent rise in goods prices over the year.
  • The rate decision: the documented mechanism is the December meeting, in which the October producer and consumer price reports are the last inflation data the Federal Open Market Committee will see before it meets December 8 and 9.
  • The contrast: the documented mechanism is the missing base month, in which the October consumer price release carried no 12-month rate because October 2025 survey data was never collected during the 2025 shutdown, while the producer price release does carry one.
  • Structural note: the September increase was revised up from 1.3 percent to 1.4 percent; revisions reflect late reports and corrections by respondents.

Sources:

  • Reuters, November 13 — largest annual gain in four years, forecast 0.4 percent, September revised to 1.4 percent, goods lifted by food and basic organic chemicals
  • CNN, November 13 — highest annual rate in nearly four years, vegetables up 26.7 percent, electronic components up 31.7 percent over the year, rate hike expected in December
