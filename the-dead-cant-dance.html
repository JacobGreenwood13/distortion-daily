Martyrdom autopsy № A5

The Dead Can't Dance

Every movement buries its founder twice: once in the ground, and once in marble. The dead make the best spokesmen. They never go off script, never embarrass you with a new opinion, never age into something inconvenient.

By Jacob · Editor · October 26, 2026 · 13 min read

THE FREEZE

Every movement buries its founder twice: once in the ground, and once in marble.

The dead make the best spokesmen. They never go off script. They never embarrass you with a new opinion. They never get caught, never recant, never age into something inconvenient. Death freezes the record. Everything a person was still becoming stops mid-sentence, and what remains gets carved down to whatever the survivors find useful. The living have to keep earning their reputation. The dead only have to keep theirs from being examined.

That is the whole mechanism. Everything below is documentation.

WHY THE MIND BUYS IT

This is psychology, and it works on everyone — including the people writing this.

Sacrifice looks like proof. When someone pays the ultimate price for a cause, the human mind reads the size of the payment as evidence for the size of the truth. Psychologists call this costly signaling: a sacrifice authenticates how deeply someone believed. The error comes one step later, when the audience quietly transfers that authenticity from the believer to the belief. He died for it, so it must be true. That does not follow. People have died for false things with total sincerity. The sincerity was real. The conclusion doesn't transfer.

Pain makes participation feel meaningful. In five experiments, researchers Christopher Olivola and Eldar Shafir found that people gave more to a cause when contributing involved pain or effort — running a marathon in freezing rain rather than attending a picnic — and that the suffering itself made the cause feel more meaningful. They called it the martyrdom effect. Scale it up from a charity run to a firing squad. If a little pain makes a cause feel meaningful, a death makes it feel sacred. The feeling is real, and the feeling is not evidence.

Death reminders harden the tribe. Terror management research has found that reminding people of death increases hostility toward critics of their worldview. A martyr's death is a permanent, built-in death reminder sitting at the center of the movement. Criticize the figure and you are not making an argument; you are threatening the worldview, and the tribe responds accordingly. (This literature has replication problems researchers are still sorting through. The pattern is worth naming; it is not worth building the whole case on.)

Institutions do the carving. The sociologist Maurice Halbwachs argued that collective memory is not a recording but a reconstruction — societies remember what their present needs them to remember, and they do it through schools, monuments, holidays, speeches, and rituals. A dead founder is raw material. The textbook, the statue, the national holiday: each one sands off another inconvenient edge until what remains is a symbol simple enough to fit on a poster.

Mourning binds. Émile Durkheim observed that shared ritual — grief included — fuses a group together around sacred symbols. A martyr's funeral is the most powerful bonding ritual a movement ever gets. The tears are real, the unity they produce is real, and the symbol at the center of it becomes untouchable by the same process that made it beloved.

The formula: a death no one can argue with, processed by minds built to sanctify sacrifice, carved by institutions that need simple symbols, bonded by grief. The result is a person-shaped saint with the rough edges removed.

Pay close attention to what gets removed. That is where the files below come in.

THE GALLERY

Six figures. What the marble says, and what's in the file.

Mohandas Gandhi. The marble: the apostle of nonviolence, father of the Indian nation. The file: in his early years in South Africa, Gandhi wrote contemptuously of Black Africans — language he outgrew but never fully answered for. Late in life he conducted "celibacy experiments" that involved sleeping beside young women, including his late-teen grandniece — arrangements that raise questions about power and consent no hagiography can dissolve. None of this erases what he built. It complicates the saint. Saints aren't supposed to have complications. That is the point of the marble.

Martin Luther King Jr. The marble: the dream speech, 1963, frozen forever at the Lincoln Memorial. The file: a Boston University committee found that large portions of his doctoral dissertation were appropriated without attribution — plagiarism by the standards of any university on earth. And the marble crops the timeline. The King of 1967–68 called the United States "the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today" and was organizing a multiracial Poor People's Campaign to shut down Washington until poverty was addressed. That King is far less quoted than the King of 1963. One of those Kings is safe to put on a stamp. Guess which.

Malcolm X. The marble: the uncompromising prophet of Black dignity. The file has two entries, and both matter. During his Nation of Islam years he dealt in sweeping racial indictments. Then came Mecca, 1964: "I am not a racist… I no longer subscribe to sweeping indictments of any one race." He was assassinated in February 1965, less than a year later. The revision never got its second act.

Abraham Lincoln. The marble: the Great Emancipator. The file: Lincoln supported and pursued plans to resettle freed Black Americans outside the United States — colonization schemes that continued into his presidency, their full extent still debated by historians. The marble skips that. It also skips where he was heading: on April 11, 1865, in his last public speech, Lincoln endorsed limited Black suffrage — for veterans and "the very intelligent." John Wilkes Booth was in the crowd. Booth reportedly said that speech would be Lincoln's last. Three days later, he made it so.

John F. Kennedy. The marble: Camelot, the brave young president cut down in Dallas. The file: his administration raised the American adviser presence in Vietnam past 16,000 and tacitly approved the 1963 coup that ended with Ngo Dinh Diem murdered. What Kennedy would have done next in Vietnam is unknowable — and because it is unknowable, every faction gets to claim the Kennedy that suits them. Death froze a trajectory and turned it into a Rorschach test.

Che Guevara. The marble: the t-shirt, the eternal rebel. The file: as commander of La Cabaña fortress after the Cuban revolution, Guevara presided over revolutionary tribunals and firing squads. The body counts vary wildly depending on who is counting and why — exile groups inflate, Havana minimizes, and every number arrives with an incentive attached. The tribunals and his command responsibility are not in serious dispute. The t-shirt doesn't mention the wall.

THE REVISION FILES

This is the section the marble fears most.

Every figure above was still thinking when death stopped the clock. Malcolm X spent his final year publicly revising the racial doctrines that made him famous — and is remembered for the doctrines, not the revision. King spent his final year radicalizing past the 1963 consensus — and the consensus is what got the holiday. Gandhi, in 1947, called himself a "spent bullet" and wrote that what he had mistaken for satyagraha was only passive resistance, "a weapon of the weak" — that he had "all along laboured under an illusion." The saint's final verdict on his own life's work was doubt. Lincoln's final speech pointed toward Black suffrage, a position beyond anything in the marble's version of him.

We do not remember these people as they were. We remember them as they were before they finished thinking. Death didn't just freeze the record — it froze a draft. And the institutions that carved the marble had every incentive to keep the earlier draft, because the earlier draft was simpler, safer, and more useful.

A living person gets to revise. A dead one gets revised.

THE CLAIM GAME

Then comes the part nobody wants to talk about: the party with the most to gain claims the body.

A dead founder is an asset with no legal department. Anyone can quote him. No one can make him stop them. So the factions move in, each carrying the same corpse to a different podium.

The American right spent years insisting Martin Luther King Jr. was a Republican — billboards, a video from his niece for the Black Republican Association, viral posts with a doctored MAGA-hat photo claiming he "would've MAGA'd today." His children denied it. His niece retracted it. King himself said he was "not inextricably bound to either party" and wrote that the 1964 GOP "geared its appeal and program to racism, reaction, and extremism." Follow the claimant's needs and the claim explains itself.

Meanwhile both parties fight over a single sentence — "judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character" — the right deploying it against affirmative action while King's documented record supported race-conscious reparative measures. One sentence, two armies, neither quoting the whole man.

In India, the BJP put Gandhi's spectacles on the Swachh Bharat logo and photographs Modi spinning the charkha — while the party's own MP praised Gandhi's assassin as a "patriot" in Parliament and kept her seat, while Godse temples rise and party messaging recasts Gandhi as "a good Hindu, almost a Hindutva icon," quietly shelving the Hindu-Muslim unity that was his central creed. Godse was an RSS man who murdered Gandhi for "pandering to Muslims." The movement that produced the assassin now wears the victim's glasses, and the glasses poll well.

Newt Gingrich invoked John F. Kennedy to sell Ronald Reagan's 1981 tax cuts, and the Wall Street Journal's orbit enshrined the 1964 Kennedy cut alongside Reagan's. Kennedy's cuts were Keynesian demand-side measures aimed at putting money in wage earners' pockets — the opposite theory wearing the same numbers. The corpse couldn't correct the citation.

And the modern GOP calls itself the Party of Lincoln — Lincoln of the federal income tax, the greenbacks, the tariffs, the transcontinental railroad built on federal land grants and federal power. The name is the asset. The record is negotiable.

Here is the tell, and it works every time: when a party quotes the martyr, ask what the martyr said about that party — and what the party left out. If two opposed camps both claim the same dead man, at least one of them is using a corpse as a spokesperson. Usually it's both.

THE LIVING

Now the control group: what happens when the saint survives long enough to generate new data.

Aung San Suu Kyi spent fifteen years under house arrest as the world's most famous democracy icon — Nobel Prize, the face of peaceful resistance. Then she took power, and in December 2019 she stood before the International Court of Justice and defended Myanmar's military against charges of genocide against the Rohingya. The icon didn't change. The data did. The living get second acts, and second acts ruin marble.

Mother Teresa was canonized a saint in the formal, Vatican-certified sense — the rare case where the marble got its paperwork. The file, assembled most fully in a 2013 peer-reviewed study by three Canadian researchers that analyzed 287 documents, alleges squalid hospice conditions, associations with dictators and fraudsters, millions in donations with thin accounting, and a theology that prized suffering over relief. Her defenders dispute much of it, and the dispute itself is the point: she lived long enough, and publicly enough, for a file to exist at all. The living get files. The dead get feast days.

That is the difference survival makes. The living keep producing evidence. Evidence is the enemy of sainthood.

THE WEAPON

Every mechanism in this piece has a final form, and it is a law.

In Turkey, publicly insulting the memory of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk — the founder, dead since 1938 — is a crime punishable by one to three years in prison under Law 5816. A professor received a 15-month suspended sentence for questioning the scale of the Atatürk cult. Thousands have been prosecuted under the law. The symbol has become a statute.

This is where the freeze ends: disagreement reclassified as desecration, desecration reclassified as crime. The dead founder becomes a legal instrument, and the instrument points at the living. Question the record, and the state answers. The marble has a police force.

Every country has its softer version — the holiday that forbids the footnote, the textbook that skips the file, the accusation of disrespect that ends the argument before it starts. Turkey just wrote it down.

CLOSE

So judge the argument, not the halo.

A cause that needs a flawless founder is a cause that cannot survive inspection, and a movement that answers criticism with accusations of disrespect has told you exactly what it fears. The dead deserve better than worship. They deserve the truth — the whole file, the revisions, the doubts, the parts the marble left out. Worship is what you give someone when you've stopped listening to them.

The martyrs were people. People are complicated. Anyone selling you a simple one is selling you something.

FOLLOW THE INCENTIVES

Every study and source above was made by someone who benefits if you believe it. A few worth naming:

  • Olivola & Shafir (martyrdom effect): academic psychologists publishing in an open-access journal. No commercial funder, no product to sell. The risk isn't corruption — it's over-application, which is why the article leans on it lightly.
  • Terror management researchers: same — academic, but the subfield has live replication disputes. Treated as suggestive, not settled.
  • FactCheck.org: run by the Annenberg Public Policy Center. Its currency is credibility as a neutral arbiter — which is an incentive to be careful, and also an incentive to appear above the fight.
  • Sagarika Ghose (Gandhi/Godse column): Indian journalist and Modi-government critic. Her incentive is oppositional — but the load-bearing facts (Thakur's remarks, Modi's response, the Swachh Bharat branding) are corroborated by wire reporting cited alongside.
  • AMAC (cited for the right's use of King): a conservative seniors organization. Its incentive is ideological — which is exactly why it's cited as evidence of the phenomenon, not as an authority on what King believed.
  • The Cuba Archive (Che body counts): an anti-Castro exile organization. Maximum incentive to inflate. That's why the article asserts only command responsibility — the one fact both sides' incentives can't erase — and treats every number as a claim with an owner.
  • Berkeley law review / ACLU (King's actual positions): academic and advocacy sources with pro-race-conscious-policy incentives. Cited for King's documented positions, corroborated across both.
