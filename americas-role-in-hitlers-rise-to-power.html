America's Role in Hitler's Rise to Power

This article traces the road that handed the Nazis their power — and names who helped dig the crater: America, American money, American mismanagement.

By Jacob · Editor · November 23, 2026 · 23 min read

THE BUBBLE AMERICA BLEW

In the 1920s, buying stocks became a national pastime. Ordinary people poured into the market with little understanding of how it worked, and brokers handed them a loaded tool called buying on margin: put up 10 percent of a stock's price, borrow the other 90 percent. As long as prices climbed, everyone got rich on borrowed money. The moment prices fell more than 10 percent, the broker issued a margin call, and the investor was wiped out in minutes — owing money on top of losing everything.

The Federal Reserve, created in 1913 to keep the financial system stable, kept interest rates low while the speculation ran wild, then tightened credit in 1929 just as the economy was already softening. Factories were producing more goods than people could buy. Farms were drowning in overproduction. Consumer debt was stacked to the ceiling on installment plans. The bubble was the predictable result of a financial system that let speculation run unchecked and then pulled the rug at the worst possible moment.

In October 1929 the rug got pulled. Between Black Thursday (October 24) and mid-November, the Dow Jones lost almost half its value. It kept falling until July 1932, bottoming 89 percent below its peak, and did not regain its pre-crash heights until November 1954 — a quarter century later. A 1932 Senate investigation, the Pecora Commission, later dug through the wreckage and exposed the fraud and abuse inside the banks that had sold the bubble to the public. The speculation was reckless and rotten. That is the American half of the story: a crash built out of American speculation, American borrowed money, and American regulatory failure.

THE TREATY THAT PLANTED THE BOMB

The road starts in 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. The treaty that ended World War I pinned sole responsibility for the war on Germany — Article 231, the "war guilt" clause — and built the punishment on top of it. Reparations were set at 132 billion gold marks, fixed in gold marks, a currency Germany could not print, which meant the debt could not be inflated away. Germany lost roughly 13 percent of its territory: Alsace-Lorraine back to France, the Polish corridor splitting the country, the Saar placed under foreign control, every overseas colony stripped. The army was capped at 100,000 men, with no air force, no submarines, and a demilitarized Rhineland.

The German public called it a Diktat — a dictated peace — and never accepted its legitimacy. The politicians who signed it were branded traitors. Out of that humiliation grew the "stab-in-the-back" legend: the claim, spread by nationalists and later adopted by the Nazis, that the German army had never been defeated in the field but betrayed at home by socialists, democrats, and Jews. It was baseless. It was also politically devastating. The treaty manufactured the grievance that Hitler would spend the next two decades harvesting.

THE YEAR MONEY DIED

In January 1923, France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr — Germany's industrial heartland — after Germany fell behind on reparations deliveries. Berlin answered with passive resistance: workers downed tools, and the government paid their wages by printing money. The mark fell off a cliff. A loaf of bread that cost 250 marks in January cost roughly 200 billion marks by November. By autumn, one American dollar bought 4.2 trillion marks.

Hyperinflation is a transfer. Debtors were freed; anyone holding cash, savings, pensions, or bonds was wiped out. The people destroyed were the careful, salaried middle class — the ones who had done exactly what the state asked of them — watching a lifetime of thrift dissolve in months. Historians widely regard the episode as a lasting engine of the era's political radicalization. The middle class never forgave the republic for it.

On November 15, 1923, the government issued the Rentenmark: one Rentenmark for one trillion paper marks, with a hard cap on printing. The panic stopped almost immediately. The currency was saved. The trust was not.

THE WIRE THAT RAN TO BERLIN

Germany's roaring twenties were financed by the United States.

After World War I, Germany owed crushing reparations. In 1924, the Dawes Plan restructured those payments and opened the lending tap: American banks, led by J.P. Morgan & Co., organized an initial loan of 800 million gold marks — about $200 million — and the money kept flowing. By the time the world economy cracked in 1929, Germany was running on foreign credit. The German recovery was real — industrial output doubled, the middle class stabilized — but it was built on borrowed money, and most of those loans were short-term. That meant the lenders could call them in at any moment.

When Wall Street collapsed, American banks did exactly that. They pulled their money home to cover their own losses. The credit that held Germany up vanished in months. And the collapse has names and dates. On May 11, 1931, Austria's largest bank, Creditanstalt, announced it could not publish its accounts — the first domino. On June 6, Germany announced it could not meet its reparations payments as planned. On June 20, President Hoover proposed a one-year moratorium on reparations and war debts; France, which needed German reparations to service its own debts, resisted, and the moratorium did not take effect until July 6 — after the damage was done. On July 13, Danatbank, one of Germany's largest banks, failed when its loan to the struggling textile company Nordwolle went bad. Panic swept the country. The government declared a bank holiday on July 14. On July 15, the Reichsmark was cut loose from the gold standard and capital controls slammed down. Unemployment climbed toward 30 percent — around 6 million people out of work. Industrial production fell roughly 42 percent. The government answered with brutal austerity under Chancellor Brüning, which squeezed a starving population even harder.

As the earlier article in this collection showed, America has wrestled with national debt since its first days. In 1790, Alexander Hamilton asked the new republic to honor about $79 million in Revolutionary debts, arguing that properly funded debt would be "a national blessing." His insight was simple: a country's money is worth what the world believes its promises are worth. Pledge real revenue behind the debt, service it without fail, and the nation's credit — its global worth — rises. After his plan passed, American securities tripled in value, because the market believed. Break any link in that chain, and the blessing becomes a curse. Germany lived the curse in full: reparations it could not pay, a currency annihilated in the hyperinflation of 1923, then a recovery built entirely on other people's money. When the loans vanished, there was no credit left to fall back on — no pledged revenue, no believed promise. A nation whose money the world no longer trusts is a nation with nothing left to lose, and nothing-left-to-lose is the most flammable material in politics.

THE TARIFF WALL

And while the credit system burned, Washington poured gasoline on trade. In June 1930, Congress passed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, sharply raising American import duties — the highest American tariff of the century. European nations retaliated with duties of their own. World trade collapsed by roughly two-thirds between 1929 and 1934. American export volume fell 49 percent between 1929 and 1932, driven chiefly by foreign retaliation. Economists note the Depression's income collapse drove most of the trade decline — the tariff compounded it, not caused it alone.

For Germany, the timing was a death sentence. Germany needed to earn dollars through exports to service the very debts America was demanding — and America had just slammed the door on the goods that could have paid them. The economist Paul Samuelson later marveled at the spectacle: a creditor nation strangling the trade that was its debtors' only way to pay. Some historians go further, arguing that the tariff's strangling of the world economy fed the political extremism that carried Hitler to power. Our read: the tariff carries its own share of the guilt. America built the loan machine, yanked the credit, and then blockaded the trade that could have softened the fall — three American decisions, each documented, each landing on the same ruined population.

WHAT DEBT DOES TO TRADE

There is a second half to Hamilton's insight, and it runs through every shipping lane on earth. When a country's debts pile up to the point that the world starts doubting its promises, investors sell its currency, and the currency weakens. Each unit of it buys less on the global market. That hits trade from both sides.

Imports get more expensive. Foreign sellers price their goods in their own money. When your currency weakens, you hand over more of it for the same barrel of oil, the same electronics, the same shipment of grain. Japan lived a mild version of this recently: as the yen slid, import prices measured in yen ran nearly 30 percent higher than the same goods measured in the exporters' own currencies — the gap was almost entirely the weaker currency.

Exports lose global worth from the other direction. When your money weakens, foreigners pay less of their own money for each thing you make. The goods still ship — they may even ship in greater volume, since they look cheap abroad — but every unit of real work your country exports commands less global purchasing power in return. The world discounts your output.

Weimar Germany lived the extreme of both at once. When the mark collapsed, imported food and raw materials became impossibly expensive while German labor and goods sold abroad for pennies. A country that cannot command fair worth for what it makes, and cannot afford what it must buy, is a country being stripped by the terms of trade itself — before a single shot is fired.

Our read: this is where American responsibility stops being abstract. The United States exported the crash through a lending machine it built, into an economy it made dependent, and then yanked the cord. None of that excuses what Germans did next. It explains the size of the crater they were standing in.

WHAT CRUSHED PEOPLE DO

A person who loses everything to forces they cannot see does not sit down and write an essay about monetary policy. They look for someone to blame. That reflex is human.

Germany in 1932 was a country of humiliated veterans, ruined shopkeepers, and starving workers — people who had watched their savings evaporate twice in a decade (first the hyperinflation of 1923, then the Depression). The Weimar government offered them austerity and lectures. The Communists offered them revolution, which terrified the middle class. Hitler offered them something simpler: a story. Germany was not failing; Germany was being betrayed. And the betrayers had faces he could point to.

This is the injustice-to-target pipeline, and it runs the same way in every country. Economic ruin creates a hunger for explanation. A demagogue supplies the explanation with a villain attached. The villain does not have to be guilty. The villain has to be visible.

THE NUMBERS OF THE SURGE

Watch what the crater did to the vote. In May 1928, before the crash, the Nazi Party won 2.6 percent of the vote — a fringe sect. In September 1930, with the Depression biting, they jumped to 18.3 percent. In July 1932, with unemployment at its worst, they hit 37.3 percent — the largest party in the Reichstag, and the highest share they would ever win in a free election.

In November 1932, their vote actually slipped to 33.1 percent. The wave was receding. Hitler did not ride a popular majority into power. In January 1933, conservative elites — Franz von Papen and President Hindenburg chief among them — appointed him chancellor through backroom dealing, convinced they could control him and use his movement against the left. They could not. Within months, every constraint was gone: opposition parties banned, press crushed, the whole machinery of the state in his hands.

Our read: the German people voted for a man who promised to end their humiliation, in numbers the Depression manufactured. The Holocaust came later, through the power the elites handed him.

WHY ORDINARY PEOPLE WENT ALONG

Three experiments, decades apart, explain the machinery better than any speech.

In 1951, the psychologist Solomon Asch put a single volunteer in a room with a group of strangers and asked everyone to match the length of a line — a task with an obvious correct answer. The strangers were confederates, instructed to give the same wrong answer. About 75 percent of the real participants went along with the group's wrong answer at least once; across all trials, the conformity rate was about 37 percent. Most of them later admitted they knew the group was wrong — they just did not want to look peculiar. When just one confederate broke ranks and gave the right answer, conformity collapsed to about 5 percent. One dissenter was enough to free everyone else.

In the early 1960s, the psychologist Stanley Milgram ran a series of experiments at Yale University on obedience to authority. A volunteer was told to teach word pairs to a "learner" in another room — secretly an actor — and to punish every wrong answer with an electric shock, raising the voltage each time, up to 450 volts. The learner was never actually shocked, but the volunteer heard him scream. Whenever the volunteer hesitated, a man in a lab coat calmly told him to continue.

Sixty-five percent of participants went all the way to 450 volts. Every single one went to at least 300. Milgram's explanation was the "agentic state": under a perceived authority figure, people stop experiencing themselves as responsible actors and start experiencing themselves as instruments — their personal agency handed over to the man in the lab coat. The authority absorbs the responsibility, and the conscience goes quiet.

The variations proved the authority was the switch. When the orders came by telephone instead of from a man standing in the room, full obedience collapsed to about one in five. When the volunteer had to force the learner's hand onto the shock plate himself, it fell to 30 percent. Remove the authority's presence, and the obedience drains away with it.

Milgram's "agentic state" explanation is itself debated. Some psychologists argue the volunteers were not surrendering their agency so much as identifying with the experiment's goals — doing what they believed the science required. The history carries the same dispute: many of the regime's servants acted out of conviction, not mere obedience. The likeliest truth is that obedience and belief worked together — which only sharpens the point, because the conditions America helped build fed both.

In 1971, Philip Zimbardo turned a Stanford basement into a mock prison and randomly assigned 24 psychologically healthy college students to be guards or prisoners. The guards got uniforms and mirrored sunglasses — anonymity, what psychologists call deindividuation, the loss of self-awareness inside a group role. The prisoners got numbers instead of names. The study was supposed to run two weeks. Within days the guards were subjecting prisoners to psychological torment and humiliation, and Zimbardo shut the whole thing down after six. His conclusion: the roles and the situation overrode the personalities. Ordinary students became cruel the moment the uniform told them cruelty was the job. The experiment's methods have been heavily criticized — coaching, demand characteristics, selective reporting — so treat its broadest conclusion as contested, not settled. But the narrower pattern, that a role can license cruelty, keeps showing up in the record.

Put the three together and each one names a different face of the regime. Asch is the crowds — the rallies, the sea of raised arms, the neighbors who conformed because everyone around them conformed. The German public, its judgment silenced by the group. Milgram is the soldiers — the men who operated the machinery, their personal agency handed up the chain of command to the officer giving the order. Obedience as doctrine, conscience outsourced to rank. Zimbardo is the corrupt authority — the camp guards, the SS men, the party functionaries whose uniforms and titles dissolved the person inside until cruelty was simply the job. The group takes your judgment. The authority takes your agency. The role takes your self. Germany in the 1930s ran all three at national scale, with real uniforms and real prisons.

THE EXCEPTIONS THAT PROVE THE RULE

Here is the strangest documented fact in this whole story. For all of Hitler's raving about Jews as a category, he personally exempted individual Jews he knew.

Dr. Eduard Bloch was the Jewish physician who treated Hitler's mother as she died of breast cancer in 1907, charging the struggling family little or nothing. Hitler never forgot it. Years later he called Bloch an "Edeljude" — a noble Jew — and reportedly said that if all Jews were like him, there would be no Jewish question. After Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Bloch received Gestapo protection on Hitler's orders and was allowed to emigrate to the United States with his family.

Lieutenant Hugo Gutmann was the Jewish officer who, as Hitler's direct superior in 1918, recommended him for the Iron Cross First Class — the medal Hitler wore for the rest of his life. Gutmann kept his military pension under the Nazis, was released after being arrested by the Gestapo during Kristallnacht, and was allowed to emigrate to America in 1940.

Heinrich Himmler himself later mocked the pattern, sneering that every German seemed to have "his decent Jew" — one personal exception to the hatred. That is the tell. The ideology needed Jews as an abstract category, a faceless villain for the story. The moment a Jew became a person the leader actually knew, the category broke down — and the machine murdered millions of strangers anyway. The hatred was never about the individuals. It was about the need for a target.

THE DOORS THAT STAYED SHUT

America helped build the conditions for the rise — and then closed the exits once the machine started moving. When the victims came looking for a door, it shut that too. In July 1938, thirty-two nations gathered at Évian, France, to discuss the Jewish refugee crisis. Almost none of them raised their immigration quotas. The United States did not even fill the quota it already had.

On May 13, 1939, the ocean liner St. Louis left Hamburg carrying 937 passengers, nearly all of them Jewish refugees fleeing Germany after Kristallnacht. Cuba admitted 28 and turned the rest away. The ship sailed slowly toward Miami. Telegrams begging for refuge went to the White House and the State Department. Entry was denied, and a U.S. Coast Guard vessel shadowed the ship to make sure no desperate passenger swam ashore. On June 6, the St. Louis turned back toward Europe. Four countries divided the passengers at Antwerp on June 17. When Germany invaded western Europe the following year, hundreds were trapped under Nazi rule again — 254 of those sheltered in Belgium, France, and the Netherlands died at Nazi hands.

The American quota for Germany and Austria stood at 27,370 visas a year. In 1933, the year Hitler took power, it was 5.3 percent filled. In 1938, the year of Kristallnacht, about 65 percent. Consular officials buried applicants in paperwork — identity documents, police certificates, financial affidavits, each with expiration dates, each obtainable only in a particular order — while the Nazis made the documents impossible to get. The quota was finally filled in 1939, the first time since the Nazis rose to power, and nearly filled in 1940. By then the doors that mattered had been shut for six years. Accountability covers what a country does and what it refuses to do while people are drowning in the consequences.

THE MIRROR

Americans tell themselves a story: America was founded on good values, so America is fundamentally good, so American leaders acting in America's name must be serving something decent. That story shields Americans the same way the German myths shielded Germans. It is a permission slip. It lets citizens outsource their judgment to the flag and stop watching what the people in charge actually do.

The Germans who ushered in Hitler were ruined, frightened, ordinary people who wanted their dignity back and stopped asking hard questions of the man who promised it. Asch showed what happens when nobody in the room dissents. Milgram showed what happens when a perceived authority strips people of their personal agency. Zimbardo showed what happens when the role replaces the person. The German public lived all three findings — and the price was paid by millions of people who had done nothing.

And the parallel runs both directions. The German people welcomed Hitler because they were angry and hurt and he promised the pain would end. The American people did the mirror image: they welcomed, voted for, and cheered the very leaders whose speculation and borrowed money built the bubble and dug the crater — at home and, through those loans, in Germany too. The men who mismanaged the American economy into the ground were ushered in by the public, the same way. Every people gets the leaders it stops questioning.

America's financial mismanagement built the crater that Hitler filled. That is a matter of record: the speculation, the margin debt, the yanked loans, the foreign credit Germany depended on, the 6 million unemployed, the 37.3 percent. Americans do not get to wave the founding values at that record like a shield. Good values at the founding are a standard, and a standard you do not enforce is decoration.

So here is the accountability, public and personal. Public: when leaders gamble the economy on speculation and borrowed money, the bill does not land on them — it lands on ordinary people, and desperate ordinary people are the raw material of demagogues. That is true in every country, including this one. Personal: Asch's one dissenter is the whole lesson. The spell breaks when a single person in the room says the line is not the length everyone claims. Conformity is a choice, even when it does not feel like one. The uniform only replaces your conscience if you hand it over.

The Germans learned this at a cost of millions of lives. America paid for the lesson once, in 1929, in money. The question is whether it will learn it before paying in something worse. Pay close attention — because the machinery has not changed. Only the uniforms are different.
