1. Senate advances the shutdown deal 60 to 40 as the government enters its 40th day

What happened: On Sunday night, November 8, the Senate voted 60 to 40 to invoke cloture on the motion to proceed with a bipartisan funding package, breaking the impasse that had produced 14 failed cloture votes on the House-passed continuing resolution. The shutdown that began on October 1 entered its 40th day on Monday, November 9, and remains the longest in United States history. Eight members of the Senate Democratic caucus voted to advance the package: Dick Durbin of Illinois, John Fetterman of Pennsylvania, Maggie Hassan and Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire, Tim Kaine of Virginia, Catherine Cortez Masto and Jacky Rosen of Nevada, and Angus King of Maine, an independent who caucuses with Democrats. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer voted against advancing it. Rand Paul of Kentucky was the only Republican to vote no. The package uses the House-passed bill as its vehicle and combines it with three full-year appropriations bills covering the Agriculture Department and the Food and Drug Administration, military construction and the Department of Veterans Affairs, and the legislative branch, funded through September 30, 2027; the rest of the government would be funded through January 30, 2027. It fully funds the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program through the end of the fiscal year, guarantees back pay for federal workers, reinstates workers fired during the shutdown, and bars further reductions in force through January 30. It does not extend the Affordable Care Act subsidies that expire at the end of December; Majority Leader John Thune committed on the Senate floor to a separate vote on the subsidies no later than the second week of December, with no guaranteed outcome. The vote does not reopen the government: the Senate must still complete debate and pass the package, and the House, which has not been in session since September 19, must pass it before it reaches the president. House members were notified on Sunday night that they will receive 36 hours' notice to return, and Speaker Mike Johnson said on Monday that the House plans to vote by Wednesday.

Incentives:

  • The eight Democratic-caucus votes: the documented mechanism is the 60-vote cloture threshold, under which the package could not advance until eight members of the caucus joined nearly all Republicans.
  • The Democratic leadership: the documented mechanism is the subsidy condition, in which the caucus held funding support for an extension of Affordable Care Act subsidies and accepted a promised December vote instead of a guaranteed extension.
  • The majority leader: the documented mechanism is the floor commitment, in which Thune promised a subsidies vote no later than the second week of December in exchange for the votes to advance the package.
  • The fired workers: the documented mechanism is the reinstatement and back-pay language, in which the package reverses reductions in force carried out during the shutdown and bars further ones through January 30.
  • Structural note: the Sunday vote was cloture on the motion to proceed, not final passage; the government stays closed until the Senate passes the package, the House passes it, and the president signs it.

2. Two Senate races still uncalled as Alaska prepares its next count and Mississippi counts affidavit ballots

What happened: As of Monday morning, November 9, the two remaining uncalled Senate races from the November 3 midterm election had not moved since Friday. In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola led Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 votes in first-choice results, 102,962 to 102,325, with about 74 percent of ballots counted; roughly 60,000 early and absentee ballots were still uncounted, with the next count due November 9 or 10. No candidate holds a majority, so the outcome depends on ranked-choice tabulation scheduled for November 18, and the state must certify by November 25. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes, 467,930 to 465,356, with an estimated 96.9 percent of votes counted as of Friday, November 6; the Mississippi secretary of state estimated 12,000 to 20,000 affidavit ballots were still uncounted. Mail ballots may arrive through November 10, counties must certify by November 13, and the secretary of state declares the final result on December 3. Mississippi has no recount law. The Senate stands at 54 Democrats to 44 Republicans with the two races uncalled.

Incentives:

  • The Alaska count: the documented mechanism is ranked-choice tabulation, in which no candidate with a majority triggers a second round counting voters' next preferences, scheduled for November 18.
  • The Mississippi count: the documented mechanism is the affidavit-ballot process, in which 12,000 to 20,000 ballots remain uncounted against a 2,574-vote margin, with counties certifying by November 13.
  • Structural note: race calls are projections based on counted votes and remaining ballots, not certifications; neither race has been called by the Associated Press.

Sources:

  • SRN News, November 6 — both races uncalled, Peltola lead of 600 votes, Colom lead of about 1,800 votes at that count

3. Herzog tasks Eisenkot with forming Israel's government, starting a 28-day clock

What happened: On Sunday, November 8, President Isaac Herzog formally tasked Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, with forming a government at a ceremony at the President's Residence in Jerusalem. Herzog announced the decision on Saturday, November 7, after consultations in which factions representing 67 members of the 120-seat Knesset recommended Eisenkot, factions representing 46 recommended Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, and the seven members of United Torah Judaism declined to recommend either candidate. Under Basic Law: The Government, Eisenkot has 28 days to form a government, with a possible presidential extension of up to 14 days. Netanyahu remains prime minister while the process runs. Eisenkot said he will seek a national emergency government. The new Knesset is scheduled to be sworn in on November 10. The 67 recommendations do not amount to a coalition: the Joint List, whose 11 members were among the recommendations, said it will not join a government Eisenkot leads, and Eisenkot's bloc of 48 seats needs ultra-Orthodox backing to reach the 61-seat majority.

Incentives:

  • The president: the documented mechanism is the consultation and mandate process, in which Herzog tasks the lawmaker with the strongest prospects of securing parliamentary support, with recommendations from parties not binding on the president.
  • The blocs: the documented mechanism is coalition arithmetic, in which 48 plus 7 plus 6 reaches 61, so Eisenkot's bloc needs ultra-Orthodox backing to reach a majority.
  • United Torah Judaism: the documented mechanism is the draft-law dispute, in which the party withheld its recommendation over legislation on yeshiva student exemptions and conditioned future support on resolving it.
  • Structural note: receiving the mandate is an opportunity to negotiate a government, not an appointment as prime minister; no coalition agreement has been announced.

Sources:

  • All Israel News, November 8 — national emergency government, 28-day deadline

4. Russia's grid campaign intensifies as United States-mediated talks open November 10 and 11 in Abu Dhabi

What happened: Russian strikes on Ukraine's energy infrastructure intensified over the weekend ahead of the working-level negotiations scheduled for November 10 and 11 in Abu Dhabi. Overnight into November 7, Russia launched 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones across Kyiv and 12 other regions; Ukrainian air defense shot down 4 of the missiles, and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the low interception rate reflected a shortage of Patriot interceptor missiles. Nine people were injured, including a child. The strikes damaged substations carrying electricity from the Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear power plants to Kyiv and central Ukraine, damaged homes and a kindergarten in Kyiv, and damaged Kyiv's Northern Bridge, which was closed for repairs. Zelenskyy said preliminary findings from impact sites pointed to North Korean KN-23 ballistic missiles. Overnight into November 8, Russia launched 164 drones, 112 of them jet-powered, and three Kh-59/69 cruise missiles; air defense shot down or suppressed 137 drones, with hits recorded at 23 locations. More than 600,000 customers lost power in Dnipropetrovsk Oblast and about 250,000 in Kharkiv Oblast, with outages also reported in Zhytomyr, Rivne, Odesa, and Chernihiv oblasts. On the diplomatic track, United States Special Envoy Steve Witkoff announced on November 2 that the Abu Dhabi talks will be led on the American side by Witkoff and Jared Kushner and on the Russian side by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky; it is the first trilateral round since February, and European governments will not take part.

Incentives:

  • The Russian military: the documented mechanism is the strike campaign on energy infrastructure at the start of the residential heating season, in which substations and transmission lines are hit to force nuclear plants to reduce output and cut power to hundreds of thousands of customers.
  • The Ukrainian grid operator: the documented mechanism is the emergency-outage system, in which Ukrenergo cancels or maintains cuts region by region as damage and restoration change.
  • The talks: the documented mechanism is the working-level format in Abu Dhabi, in which the two sides negotiate post-war security arrangements, de-escalation, and humanitarian steps without European governments at the table.
  • Structural note: the missile-interception figure and the North Korean missile identification come from the Ukrainian president's statements and preliminary findings, not from an independent verification of the impact sites.

  • Associated Press, November 7 — 36 ballistic missiles, 4 intercepted, Patriot shortage, 172 drones, 9 injured, North Korean KN-23 findings

5. Tankers hit in the Strait of Hormuz as Trump says new strikes are unlikely while talks progress

What happened: On November 8, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps said two oil tankers caught fire after striking naval mines while crossing what Iran calls an unauthorized route in the Strait of Hormuz; the semi-official Fars news agency later reported a third tanker had been hit and caught fire. Reuters reported it was unable to confirm the strikes, and the Iranian military did not identify the tankers' flags or destinations. The Strait of Hormuz normally carries about one-fifth of global oil consumption. On November 7, the United States military said its forces fired on and disabled a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman as it attempted to breach the blockade on Iranian ports. On Sunday, November 8, President Donald Trump said he was very unlikely to order new attacks on Iran as long as talks to end the conflict were progressing, while warning he could still strike. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called Iran's demands unrealistic. Iran has submitted a nuclear proposal to Washington through Doha, which the United States is reviewing, and Qatar said both sides were continuing to work toward an agreement. Iran's stated position remains that the strait reopens only after the United States accepts its seven conditions, including an end to the port blockade, a waiver on Iranian oil sales, and cancellation of new sanctions. Brent crude traded near $99 a barrel, down about 15 percent on the week as traders weighed the prospect of talks.

Incentives:

  • Tehran: the documented mechanism is the strait itself, in which Iran ties reopening the waterway to an end to the port blockade, a waiver on oil sales, and cancellation of new sanctions.
  • Washington: the documented mechanism is the choice between renewed strikes and continued talks, in which the president said strikes were unlikely while negotiations progress.
  • The oil market: the documented mechanism is the price response, in which Brent fell about 15 percent on the week as traders priced in the prospect of talks.
  • Structural note: the tanker incidents are Iranian claims reported by Reuters and other outlets, not independently confirmed; no vessel names, flags, operators, or casualties were confirmed in the reporting.

6. Markets bounce on Friday and close the week lower as October inflation data looms

What happened: On Friday, November 6, United States stocks closed mixed after the week's selloff: the Nasdaq Composite rose 1.09 percent to 24,579.42 and the S&P 500 gained 0.31 percent to 7,376.86, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 0.46 percent, or 237.33 points, to 51,285.73. For the week, the S&P 500 fell 2.7 percent, the Nasdaq Composite fell 3.4 percent, and the Dow fell 0.9 percent. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at about 4.86 percent after the October jobs report, and December rate-hike odds rose to about 78 percent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the October jobs report on November 6: nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 against about 65,000 expected in the Reuters poll; the unemployment rate fell to 4.1 percent from 4.2 percent; September was revised up to 60,000 and August to 186,000; average hourly earnings rose 0.4 percent to $38.00, up 3.1 percent from a year earlier. The next scheduled data point is the October consumer price index, due Tuesday, November 10. The federal funds target stands at 3.75 to 4.00 percent, held at the October 28 meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee, which meets next on December 8 and 9.

Incentives:

  • The data-center buildout: the documented mechanism is the reassessment, in which the Democratic midterm sweep and the local limits approved on November 3 changed the expected pace of approvals and capital spending on artificial intelligence infrastructure.
  • The jobs report: the documented mechanism is the data itself, in which payrolls, wages, and revisions feed into the Federal Reserve's rate decision at its December 8 and 9 meeting.
  • The bond market: the documented mechanism is the move to safety, in which the 10-year yield fell as stocks sold off, then rose to about 4.86 percent after the jobs report.
  • Structural note: the Nasdaq's 11.9 percent decline from its October 6 record close passes the 10 percent threshold that defines a correction.
