The Incentivization of Linguistic Alteration

Merriam-Webster defines "literally" two ways: in a literal sense, actually — and its opposite, in effect, virtually. The dictionary surrendered to the majority. Words are being stretched, flipped, and hollowed out on purpose, by people who profit from the confusion.

By Jacob · Editor · November 16, 2026 · 14 min read

Open a dictionary. Merriam-Webster defines "literally" two ways. The first: in a literal sense, actually. The second: in effect, virtually — used to emphasize a statement that is not literally true. The dictionary defines the word as its own opposite. It did that because enough people used it wrong for long enough, and dictionaries record usage — they follow the crowd instead of handing down the law. When the misuse becomes the majority, the misuse becomes a definition.

That surrender is the entire story of this article. Words are being stretched, flipped, and hollowed out on purpose, by people who profit from the confusion. There is a science to how it happens, a business model underneath it, and a history going back thousands of years. Pay close attention, because once you see the machinery, you will see it everywhere.

THE SCIENCE OF STRETCHING WORDS

In 2016, a psychologist named Nick Haslam published a paper titled "Concept Creep: Psychology's Expanding Concepts of Harm and Pathology," with a simple observation: harm-related words keep getting bigger. He called it concept creep. "Abuse" once meant physical and sexual violence; it now covers emotional abuse and neglect, and things get labeled neglect today that were normal parenting a generation ago. "Bullying" once meant intentional, repeated aggression by someone with more power; the intent requirement got relaxed until a person could be called a bully without ever meaning harm. "Trauma" once meant battlefield-level horror; it now covers vicarious and non-life-threatening experiences. "Prejudice" once meant blatant hatred; it now includes subtle slights. Six concepts, same pattern: each one expanded through a long series of small redefinitions until the word covered far more than it started covering.

Haslam identified two directions. Words creep horizontally, spreading into new territory — bullying moved from the schoolyard to the office to the internet. And they creep vertically, stretching downward to cover milder cases — the bar keeps lowering. His warning was plain: when every bad experience gets labeled with a clinical word, the words lose their meaning and people start seeing themselves as patients instead of participants in life.

Haslam's first case studies centered on words associated with the political left. Later research found the identical pattern on the right — people stretching the definitions of words central to their own worldview, like deviance, responsibility, and terrorism. The machinery does not belong to one tribe. It belongs to anyone who discovers that a bigger word is a stronger weapon.

THE WORDS THAT LOST THEIR MEANING

"Gaslighting" was Merriam-Webster's Word of the Year in 2022. Lookups jumped 1,740 percent, and the word sat in the dictionary's top fifty searches every single day of the year. It started as the title of a 1938 play about a husband who dims the gas lights and denies it, driving his wife to doubt her own mind. It became a clinical term for sustained psychological abuse — manipulation over time that makes a victim question their memory, their perception, their sanity. Then it became a synonym for "lied to me." A member of Congress declared that an oil company was "gaslighting the public." Patients coined "medical gaslighting" for doctors who dismissed their symptoms. A word that described a specific, prolonged form of abuse now means any misleading statement by anyone with power. The dictionary added the watered-down meaning as an official second definition. The word got bigger and the crime it described got smaller.

"Fake news" ran the same play in fast-forward. In October 2016 it meant fabricated viral stories — teenagers overseas running false articles for ad money, including the famous fabrication that the Pope had endorsed a candidate. Then, in January 2017, it got picked up at a press conference and aimed at a mainstream news network. A George Washington University white paper titled "FAKE NEWS!" documented the pivot: within weeks, "fake news" stopped meaning "lies dressed as news" and started meaning "news I don't like." The emotional charge stayed exactly the same. Only the target moved. Leaders around the world copied the move against their own press within the year.

"Literally" is the purest case, because there is no politics in it at all. The dictionary entry contains sense one and its opposite, side by side, and the hyperbolic use goes back to Dickens and Twain. Nobody organized a campaign. The word just got used wrong by enough people for enough centuries. That is the baseline truth underneath all of this: language is democratic in the rawest sense. The majority wins, even when the majority is wrong.

"OBJECTIVELY"

Our read: watch for this word. When someone says "objectively," check what follows, because what follows is almost always an opinion wearing a costume. "Objectively, this is the best policy." "Objectively, anyone can see that." The word's job in the sentence is to end the discussion before it starts — to declare the speaker's view a fact so that disagreeing becomes a form of ignorance rather than a difference of mind. It is the polite version of "agree with me because I'm right." Real objectivity shows its work: the data, the method, the numbers. The word "objectively," standing alone, is usually a shortcut around the work, a way to borrow the authority of facts without producing any.

WHERE CONFLATED DEFINITIONS HAVE BEEN ABUSED BY SOCIAL GROUPS

The abuse follows a pattern. A social group takes a word with a heavy original meaning — violence meant physical harm, abuse meant cruelty, gaslighting meant sustained psychological torment. Then the group stretches the word to cover its own grievances: speech it dislikes becomes "violence," disagreement becomes "gaslighting," critical reporting becomes "fake news." The trick is in what gets kept and what gets swapped. The moral weight of the original meaning stays — nobody wants to be seen defending "violence" or "abuse" — while the definition quietly becomes the stretched one. Disagreement gets upgraded to violence. Critics get upgraded to abusers. And anyone who points out the stretch gets condemned under the original meaning, as if questioning the word games were the same as defending the crime.

Our read: the conflation is the point, and it works because of the science. A 2017 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, "Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks," analyzed more than 560,000 posts about gun control, climate change, and same-sex marriage, and found that each additional moral-emotional word — "evil," "greed," "destroying," "shame," "fight" — made a post about 20 percent more likely to be shared. Words that were only moral or only emotional did not get the same boost. And the boost stayed inside like-minded networks — this language is built to electrify the home tribe. The stretched words are moral-emotional by design: "violence" hits harder than "I disagree," "gaslighting" hits harder than "you're wrong." Groups that learn the trick never go back to plain speech. Plain speech doesn't spread. The researchers said it plainly: this kind of language builds echo chambers and deepens polarization.

So the words that spread fastest are moral condemnations of the other tribe, and the groups deploying them collect the moral authority of the original definition with the reach of the stretched one. That is a property the system actively selects for, because the system runs on attention.

FOLLOW THE MONEY

Nearly every outlet's revenue — advertising, subscriptions, carriage fees — depends on capturing and holding attention. Conflict, threat, and outrage reliably beat calm, complex coverage. Newsrooms have known this since "if it bleeds, it leads." What changed is measurement. An editor can now watch, in real time, which headline framing gets more clicks, and the data quietly disciplines every future headline toward the more attention-grabbing version.

The audience noticed. In September 2025, Gallup found that 28 percent of Americans trust the media to report the news fully, accurately, and fairly — the lowest number in five decades of polling, down from over 70 percent in the 1970s. Seven in ten Americans now say they have little or no trust. The industry traded credibility for ratings, and the bill came due.

THE BRAND TRAP

Our read: none of these organizations are advertising honesty. They are advertising a brand. And once the brand exists, the only incentivized course of action is to behave as the brand advertised.

The research backs the mechanism. A 2025 paper titled "Political brand transgression," published in the International Review on Public and Nonprofit Marketing, found that voters use party brand as a shortcut — most people lack the time to evaluate every candidate, so they vote the brand the way shoppers buy the label. The study named the penalty for breaking character: brand transgression. When a brand violates the expectations it set, its own supporters punish it. A party branded as fighters must keep fighting. A network branded as the resistance must keep resisting. A network branded as the plain-truth alternative must keep performing plainness. Deviation is punished by the very audience the brand attracted, so the brand becomes a cage. The performers can see the bars. They perform anyway, because the alternative is losing the audience.

The same trap works on individuals. Researchers call it audience capture: a creator figures out what the audience rewards, the audience rewards it harder, and step by step the creator becomes the character the audience bought. A well-known video creator started with violin performances and vegan cooking, followed the audience into extreme eating videos, and the audience's appetite kept demanding more extreme performances until the character consumed the person. The incentives did it.

Fast food proves the trap has nothing to do with politics. In 1996, McDonald's spent over a hundred million dollars launching the Arch Deluxe, a "grown-up" burger aimed at adult customers, with ads showing children grimacing in disgust at it. Core customers rejected it. Adults never showed up. It was dead within a year and now sits in a museum of business failures studied in classrooms. The burger tested fine in focus groups. What failed was the contradiction: the brand was cheap, fast, family food, and the brand's customers punished the deviation. The company could see exactly what its brand allowed, and the market enforced it.

The entertainment industry runs the same machinery on personas — the tough guy, the rebel, the sweetheart — and the audience enforces the role for decades. The pattern is identical everywhere: build the brand, attract the believers, then serve the brand or lose the believers. The brand was the product, and the brand must be fed.

THE OLDEST GAME

This is one of the oldest games humans play, and philosophers have been mapping it for thousands of years.

Plato staged the first version around 360 BCE: are names natural, or are they conventions we agree on? His dialogue never settled it. John Locke, in 1689, warned that words are signs of ideas, and that using big words with no clear ideas behind them — the "abuse of words" — is a primary engine of public confusion. Wittgenstein, in the 1950s, put it sharper: the meaning of a word is its use. Language is a set of games with rules, and whoever sets the rules of the game decides what the words can mean. That sentence is worth sitting with, because it describes exactly what the definition-stretchers are doing: rewriting the rules of the game in the middle of the match.

A few years later, the philosopher J.L. Austin noticed that some sentences don't describe reality at all — they perform actions. "I pronounce you married." "I declare war." Saying it makes it so. The stretched political words work the same way. "Fake news" aimed at a real story does not describe the story; it performs an excommunication. "Threat to democracy" does not report a measurement; it performs an alarm. They are actions wearing the grammar of descriptions.

In 1946, the English writer George Orwell published an essay called "Politics and the English Language." His argument was simple and brutal. Political speech and writing, he wrote, are largely the defense of things that cannot be defended honestly — so the language has to be built from euphemism, vagueness, and fog. When villages were bombed from the air and the inhabitants driven out, the official word was "pacification." When millions of peasants were robbed of their farms, it was called "transfer of population." When people were imprisoned for years without trial or shot, it was "elimination of unreliable elements." Such phrases exist, Orwell argued, so that people can name things without picturing them. The moment you picture it, the defense collapses — so the language makes sure the picture never forms. He compared it to a cuttlefish spurting ink: when there is a gap between what someone really wants and what they claim to want, the long words and exhausted phrases come out on instinct to cover the gap.

He also caught the exact trick this article describes, eighty years early. He listed the big political words — democracy, freedom, justice, patriotic — and showed that each one carries several meanings that contradict each other, with no agreed definition that anyone will commit to. Then he wrote the sentence that matters most: these words are often used in a consciously dishonest way, where the speaker has a private definition in mind while letting the listener hear a different one. Private definition, public impression. The speaker knows what they mean; the audience hears what they want to hear; the gap between the two is where the manipulation lives.

His mechanism ran in both directions. Sloppy thinking produces sloppy language, and sloppy language makes sloppy thinking easier — each feeds the other, the way a man may drink because he feels like a failure and then fail harder because he drinks. But Orwell insisted the cycle could be broken. Ready-made phrases, he warned, will build your sentences for you and even think your thoughts for you, numbing the brain a piece at a time. His remedy was six plain rules for writers — use short words, cut every word you can cut, never dress up a simple statement — all boiling down to a single demand: let the meaning choose the word, never the other way around.

The linguist Steven Pinker named that treadmill in a 1994 New York Times piece called "The Game of the Name." People invent polite words for uncomfortable things, but the new word gets tainted by the thing it describes, so a newer word is needed, which gets tainted in turn. Water closet became toilet became bathroom became restroom became lavatory. Shell shock became battle fatigue became operational exhaustion became PTSD. Pinker's point: the underlying concept bosses the word around, never the reverse. Rename a thing and the fresh name picks up the old stains; the thing itself never gets cleaned up by renaming. Concept creep stretches old words over new territory; the treadmill abandons tainted words for new ones. Both prove the same law. The word obeys. The concept rules. And the people who understand that law can ride it like a current.

One caution from the science: language influences thought, but the strong claim — that language determines thought — does not hold up. The article claims influence, which the evidence supports, and stops there.

THE PAYOFF

In 2012, political scientists published a paper with a blunt title: "Affect, Not Ideology." Measuring decades of survey data, they found that partisans' hatred of the other side had risen dramatically since the 1990s — while actual disagreement on policy had barely moved. People did not start disagreeing more. They started disliking more. Party had become a social identity, a tribe, and the feelings followed the tribal logic: love the in-group, loathe the out-group, policy details optional.

That is the scientific spine of everything above. The definition games, the viral moral-emotional words, the brand trap — they all run on identity, not ideology. They are fighting over the words because the words are tribal weapons, and in a tribal war the side with the biggest words wins the most attention, the most outrage, and the most money.

Follow the incentives and the picture is clean. The dictionary surrenders to the majority. The majority is steered by the words that spread fastest. The words that spread fastest are moral condemnations. The condemnations are manufactured by organizations whose revenue depends on attention. The organizations are trapped by the brands they built. And the brands are defended with stretched definitions that make every disagreement sound like a crime and every opinion sound like a fact.

The fix starts with the smallest possible discipline: when someone hands you a very large word, ask what it actually means, who stretched it, and what they gain from the stretch. Words are tools. Check who is holding them, and what they are building.
