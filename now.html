The Now

The week in federal and global affairs — what happened, the incentives in play, and the sources. Refreshed regularly, never quietly rewritten.

By Jacob · Editor · Updated November 2, 2026

How to read this page

This page is a briefing. Every item gets the same three cuts: what happened (verifiable facts), incentives (who gains and through what mechanism, stated as documented structure), and sources (every claim traceable). Old editions get archived, not memory-holed.

1. Two federal judges order the Agriculture Department to use contingency funds for November SNAP benefits

What happened: On October 31, two federal judges ruled against the Agriculture Department's decision to suspend Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits for November during the government shutdown. In Boston, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani ruled that the department's refusal to use its contingency fund was based on the erroneous conclusion that the fund could not be used during a lapse in appropriations, calling the suspension contrary to law, and gave the department until November 3 to present a plan to issue at least partial payments. In Rhode Island, U.S. District Judge John McConnell Jr. issued a temporary restraining order in a case brought by cities and nonprofit organizations, giving the administration until November 3 to make full payments or until November 5 to provide a plan for partial benefits. The contingency fund holds about $4.6 billion to $5.25 billion by the figures cited in the litigation, while a full month of SNAP costs about $8.5 billion to $9 billion. The program serves about 42 million people. The orders did not by themselves load benefits onto cards on November 1, and reporting on November 1 described recipients still waiting to learn when and how much they would receive.

Incentives:

  • The Agriculture Department: the documented mechanisms are the contingency-fund decision and the department's discretion over other funding sources, including Section 32 funds, which the judges noted remain at the administration's discretion.
  • The suing states and the cities and nonprofits: the documented mechanism is the pair of federal lawsuits, which produced a Boston ruling and a Rhode Island restraining order with dated compliance deadlines of November 3 and November 5.
  • Congress: the documented mechanism is the funding lapse itself, which continues until a funding bill passes; the court orders address the contingency fund, not the appropriations gap.
  • Structural note: the fund covers roughly half a month of benefits at the cited figures, so the orders as reported establish at least partial payments, not a funded full month, unless the department taps additional sources.

Sources:

  • National Low Income Housing Coalition, October 31 rulings summary — Talwani "contrary to law" ruling and November 3 plan deadline; McConnell restraining order and November 3/November 5 deadlines
  • Boston University School of Public Health, October 31 — recipients still uncertain when and how much they would receive; 42 million recipients in every county
  • Associated Press via The Packer, October 31 — McConnell "arbitrary" finding on the $5.25 billion contingency fund; $8.5 billion to $9 billion monthly cost; 25 states and District of Columbia in the Boston case

2. Shutdown reaches day 33 as Affordable Care Act open enrollment opens November 1 with higher premiums

What happened: On November 2, the federal government shutdown reached its 33rd day, counting from October 1 under the same count used in the October 30 edition, which marked day 30. Congress has not passed funding bills for fiscal year 2026 or a continuing resolution. On November 1, open enrollment for 2027 Affordable Care Act marketplace coverage opened in most states, running through January 15, 2027, with December 15 as the deadline for coverage starting January 1. KFF reports that insurers proposed a median premium increase of 15 percent for 2027 and that finalized benchmark silver premiums rose an average of 13 percent, the second consecutive year of double-digit increases after a 26 percent rise from 2025 to 2026. The enhanced premium tax credits expired on December 31, 2025, and Congress has not renewed them, so 2027 credits follow the original formula and people with incomes above 400 percent of the federal poverty level no longer qualify for premium tax credits. KFF reports that marketplace plan selections fell by 1.2 million in the 2026 open enrollment period, that effectuated enrollment was down 12 percent, or 2.6 million people, by February 2026 to 19.2 million, and that individual market enrollment fell 18 percent by June 2026, from 25.2 million to 20.5 million people.

Incentives:

  • Congress: the documented mechanism is the 60-vote Senate threshold for a continuing resolution, which lets a minority block funding until its condition is met, while the enrollment window runs on its own statutory calendar.
  • Insurers: the documented mechanism is the filed-rate process, in which insurers set premiums from their filings; KFF found insurers attributed about four percentage points of the 2026 increase to risk-pool changes after the credit expiration, with a similar effect expected for 2027.
  • Enrollees above the subsidy threshold: the documented mechanism is the restored 400 percent income limit, which removes premium tax credits entirely for that group regardless of premium size relative to income.
  • Structural note: the 15 percent figure is the median proposed increase and the 13 percent figure is the average finalized benchmark silver increase; the two measure different stages of the rate process.

Sources:

  • KFF, 2027 open enrollment preview — November 1 start, 15 percent median proposed increase, 13 percent finalized benchmark average, credit expiration, enrollment declines
  • Center on Budget and Policy Priorities via Novo, October 27 — November 1 through January 15 window, more than 19 million marketplace enrollees, higher costs after the credit expiration
  • Political.org, November 1 — December 15 deadline for January 1 coverage, $12,000 individual out-of-pocket maximum for 2027, insurer exits in more than 20 states

3. Federal judge dismisses the Justice Department's Alaska ballot suit on November 1; the department appeals two days before the election

What happened: On November 1, U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's lawsuit over Alaska's November 3 U.S. Senate election in a 20-page order. The department, which sued on October 22, asked the court to require the state to segregate ballots ranking Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. and to block certification if incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan lost under circumstances where assigning Daniel J. Sullivan's votes to the incumbent would change the winner. Gleason ruled that the federal government lacked standing and provided no workable plan for segregating those ballots while preserving the secret ballot, and noted that Alaska law already requires federal election ballots to be preserved for 22 months. The Justice Department filed a notice of appeal on November 1; the appeal does not restore the lawsuit or order ballots set aside unless the appeals court acts. The August primary results cited in the order were Mary Peltola at 49.54 percent, Dan S. Sullivan at 41.40 percent, Daniel J. Sullivan at 2.47 percent, and Gerald Heikes at 1.05 percent. Peltola's campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee deny involvement in recruiting Daniel J. Sullivan, after the New York Times reported on October 28 that people tied to the campaign and the committee worked to recruit him. A federal criminal investigation and Governor Mike Dunleavy's state inquiry continue. Alaskans vote on November 3 in a ranked-choice election.

Incentives:

  • The court: the documented mechanism is the dismissal order, which removes the district-court obstacle to certification while leaving the appeal pending before the Ninth Circuit.
  • The Justice Department: the documented mechanism is the notice of appeal, which moves the dispute to the appeals court without itself changing ballot handling.
  • The state: the documented mechanisms are the governor's inquiry through the Department of Public Safety and the state's certification authority; the Associated Press reported the governor has suggested he may not certify a Peltola victory.
  • Structural note: the recruitment account comes from the Times report and the denials from the campaign and the committee; no finding of criminal wrongdoing has been made in these proceedings.

Sources:

  • USA Today, November 1 — dismissal, standing, secret-ballot finding, appeal notice, 53-47 Senate majority context
  • NewsCubby, November 2 update — Ninth Circuit appeal, no ballots set aside absent appeals-court action, governor's certification statement as reported by AP
  • Election Tracker, updated November 1 — primary percentages, Times report and denials, continuing criminal and state inquiries, November 3 election date

4. Israel heads into coalition consultations with no bloc at 61: Herzog begins Wednesday, Arab parties meet, recommendations line up for Eisenkot

What happened: President Isaac Herzog will begin consultations on Wednesday, November 4, with parties elected to the 120-seat Knesset to determine which lawmaker to task with forming a government, after final results gave Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats, the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot 48 seats, and Arab-led parties 19 seats. Sixty-one seats are needed for a majority. On November 1, Herzog called on party representatives to approach the process with flexibility and to find creative solutions to prevent another election. On November 2, the Joint List and the United Arab List (Ra'am) held a coordination meeting, with Joint List parliamentary bloc head Ahmad Tibi stating the objective was to replace the Netanyahu government with a government of change. The Together party, led by former Prime Minister Naftali Bennett, announced it would recommend Eisenkot, and reporting on November 2 said the Democrats were moving toward recommending Eisenkot and that Yisrael Beiteinu leader Avigdor Liberman would recommend him. If all 19 Arab lawmakers join the opposition parties in recommending Eisenkot, he would hold the backing of 67 lawmakers against 53 for Netanyahu's bloc. Separately, Israel's Kan public broadcaster reported that Netanyahu is interested in a unity government with Eisenkot, with Trump and Herzog as guarantors; that report is attributed to Kan and to a Likud confidant, not to a confirmed agreement. The results are scheduled to be submitted to Herzog on November 4, and the lawmaker tasked with forming a government will have 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension.

Incentives:

  • The blocs: the documented mechanism is coalition arithmetic — 53 plus 19 reaches 72, and 48 plus 19 reaches 67, so either bloc needs Arab-led party backing to reach 61, while Liberman has refused to form a government with Arab parties.
  • The president: the documented mechanism is the consultation and mandate process, in which Herzog tasks the lawmaker with the strongest prospects of securing parliamentary support.
  • The caretaker government: Netanyahu stays in office during negotiations, which keeps executive authority with the incumbent while talks continue.
  • Structural note: recommendations to the president are public positions, not coalition agreements; the unity-government report remains an attributed report, and no mandate has been issued yet.

Sources:

  • The Media Line via SRN News, November 1 — Wednesday consultations, Herzog "creative solutions" remarks, 53-48-19 results, Liberman refusal
  • Walaw, November 1 — consultation procedure, 28-day mandate with possible 14-day extension, caretaker status
  • Cumhuriyet Daily, November 2 — Joint List and Ra'am meeting, Together recommendation, Democrats and Liberman positions, 67-to-53 recommendation arithmetic

5. Six-day Oleshky ceasefire ends November 1 with no reported violations; strikes resume elsewhere in Ukraine and Russia

What happened: A six-day localized ceasefire around the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, in Ukraine's Kherson region, ended on November 1. The truce, in effect from October 27 to November 1, was brokered with the involvement of U.S. First Lady Melania Trump and Pope Leo, and allowed civilian evacuations from the frontline town. Nearly 800 people were evacuated over the six days; on the final day, Ukraine's military said it evacuated a further 167 civilians from the Kherson region, including 22 children and 21 people with limited mobility. Ukraine's human rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets said the ceasefire had ended, and an unnamed senior Ukrainian official told AFP that Kyiv offered an extension and Russia refused; that refusal account rests on a single Ukrainian official and the reports do not include a Russian response. On the night of November 1, Russian strikes hit energy infrastructure in the Dnipro, Vinnytsia, Zhytomyr, and Khmelnytskyi regions, and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said hundreds of thousands of households lost power. In the Vinnytsia region, a drone exploded between a residential building and a school in Koziatyn, killing three people, including a 13-year-old girl, and injuring four others. On November 2, Ukraine's General Staff said Russian forces had used the ceasefire to strengthen their military positions, a claim attributed to the Ukrainian military. Ukrainian drones struck the Lukoil refinery in Kstovo, in Russia's Nizhny Novgorod region, on the night of October 31 to November 1, setting it ablaze, according to Russian media reports cited by Al Jazeera.

Incentives:

  • The mediators: the documented mechanism is a localized, time-limited truce tied to a single humanitarian purpose — civilian evacuation — rather than a general ceasefire.
  • Both militaries: the documented mechanism is the expiration date itself, after which strikes on energy infrastructure and refineries resumed on both sides within a day.
  • The Ukrainian military: the documented mechanism is the evacuation count, which records the truce's output in civilians moved, while its November 2 statement frames the pause as also used by Russian forces to reinforce positions.
  • Structural note: the truce was limited to the Oleshky area; strikes continued elsewhere in Ukraine and Russia during the same period, and the extension-refusal account is single-sourced to a Ukrainian official.

Sources:

  • Voce Mundi, November 2 — six-day truce, no reported violations, final-day evacuations, Vinnytsia deaths, power outages
  • Zot News, November 1-2 — October 27 start, Melania Trump mediation, Zelenskyy power-outage statement, trilateral summit preparations
  • Sowetan (Reuters), November 2 — General Staff reinforcement claim, nearly 800 evacuated, 9 p.m. GMT expiration, Kherson shelling after the truce

6. UN human rights chief says U.S. boat strikes violate international human rights law and must stop

What happened: On October 31, at a press briefing in Geneva, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk said U.S. airstrikes on boats in the Caribbean and the Pacific, allegedly linked to drug trafficking, violate international human rights law and are unacceptable, and called for the attacks to halt. His spokesperson, Ravina Shamdasani, relayed the statement, saying over 60 people have reportedly been killed in the strikes since early September, that the U.S. must take all measures necessary to prevent the extrajudicial killing of people aboard the boats whatever the criminal conduct alleged against them, and that Türk called for prompt, independent, and transparent investigations. The office stated that under international human rights law, intentional lethal force is permissible only as a last resort against individuals posing an imminent threat to life, and that based on the sparse information provided publicly by U.S. authorities, none of the individuals on the targeted boats appeared to pose such a threat. The office summarized the U.S. position as arguing that the actions are governed by international humanitarian law as part of anti-drug and counter-terrorism operations, and stated that drug trafficking across borders is, as long agreed among states, a law-enforcement matter governed by human rights law limits on lethal force. Separately, on September 30, a federal judge in the Southern District of New York ordered the administration to provide, for in camera review, the Office of Legal Counsel memorandum purporting to provide the legal basis for the strikes.

Incentives:

  • The UN human rights office: the documented mechanism is the legal-frame distinction — law enforcement under human rights law versus armed conflict under humanitarian law — which determines what level of lethal force is permissible.
  • The administration: the documented mechanism is the armed-conflict designation for the campaign against cartels, which is the basis for treating the strikes as military operations rather than law-enforcement interdictions.
  • Congress and the courts: the documented mechanisms are the oversight letters from Senators Jack Reed and Roger Wicker on September 23 and October 6 seeking the strike orders and the Office of Legal Counsel opinion, and the September 30 court order for in camera review of that memorandum.
  • Structural note: the "over 60" death figure is the figure reported by the UN office on October 31; the office described the U.S. information provided publicly as sparse.

Sources:

  • UNifeed (OHCHR), October 31 — primary record of the briefing: violation finding, "unacceptable," halt call, investigations call, law-enforcement frame
  • Defense News (AP), October 31 — first UN-system condemnation of its kind, Shamdasani relay, imminent-threat standard
  • Just Security, vessel-strikes timeline — September 30 in camera order for the OLC memorandum, Reed and Wicker oversight letters, October 31 Türk statement
