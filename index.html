OCT 26

/// Lula Takes a Fourth Term From Second Place

On October 25, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won Brazil's presidential runoff with 51.8% of valid votes against 48.2% for Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, a margin of about 4.4 million votes, according to figures from Brazil's Superior Electoral Court reported by CNN and Le Monde. It is the first time since direct presidential elections returned in 1989 that the winner finished second in the first round: Bolsonaro led the first round with 47.03% to Lula's 45.16%. Bolsonaro told supporters in Brasília that he would not recognize the result and would seek annulment, citing the Supreme Court's last-minute ruling extending voting by two hours. Markets had priced the opposite outcome: Reuters reported that stocks closed on Friday almost 7% above their level going into the first round and the real nearly 5% stronger, and perpetual contracts linked to the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF fell more than 22% after Lula's victory was announced. The incentive structure is the fiscal bind awaiting him: Lula takes office on January 5 facing the most conservative Congress since Brazil's return to democracy, with Bolsonaro's party and its allies holding majorities in both chambers, public debt past 82% of GDP, and his own government's forecast of 2.3% growth next year. Investors told Reuters they want a halt to campaign-era spending and a credible path to stabilizing debt; without a working relationship with an opposition-leaning Congress, no fiscal plan passes.

OCT 26

/// The Fed Pauses, With December Still on the Table

Reuters reported October 26 that Federal Reserve policymakers have telegraphed they will hold short-term rates steady at 3.75–4.00% at the October 27–28 meeting, with rate futures pricing a 90% chance of a hold as of Friday, while traders still see a December hike as firmly possible and at least two quarter-point increases by mid-2027, according to LSEG data. The data due just after the decision explain the tension: inflation picked up last month and broadened beyond fuel prices, third-quarter GDP is expected to show 3.2% growth powered by consumer spending and AI-driven business investment, and unemployment stood at 4.2% last month. Analysts told Reuters that, were it not for a sharp rise in Treasury yields after the September hike, a second straight increase this week would have been in the cards. The 10-year yield hit 5.36% early in October and stood at 5.0% on Monday, and policymakers including New York Fed President John Williams and Vice Chair Philip Jefferson have signaled the Fed is content to wait for more data. The incentive structure is the yield curve itself: a 10-year yield at 5.0% tightens financial conditions without a vote, letting policymakers hold through the week of the midterm run-up and let the September PCE report, due Thursday, and the GDP figures carry the next decision.

OCT 26

/// Sentiment Near a Record Low Eight Days Before the Vote

The University of Michigan's Surveys of Consumers reported October 23 that its Consumer Sentiment Index edged down to 48.0 in October from a final reading of 48.1 in September, the second-lowest on record after the all-time low of 44.8 set in May, shortly after the Iran war began. Economists polled by Reuters had forecast 46.3, which was also the preliminary October reading. "Purchasing power continues to dominate consumer views of the economy," said Surveys of Consumers director Joanne Hsu; sentiment is now 10% below a year ago and 15% below February, just before the Iran conflict. Expectations for inflation over the next year nudged up to 4.7% from 4.6% in September, up from 3.4% in February, while five-year expectations rose to 3.5% from 3.4%. The decline was led by respondents identifying as independents, whose score fell to 42.4 from 47.7 in September, just above May's record-low 40.6, while scores for Democrats and Republicans improved. Millions of voters have already cast early ballots ahead of the November 3 midterms, which decide control of Congress for the final two years of President Donald Trump's term. The incentive structure is the voter bloc: the affordability election is being decided by independents whose sentiment has fallen furthest, and both parties are competing for a group whose view of the economy is now close to its worst on record.

/// Tehran Talks Pull Oil Off Its Highs, for a Day

On October 26, Brent crude fell about 1.7% to around $114.50 a barrel and U.S. crude fell about 2% to around $101, after Brent gained 6% last week to its highest weekly close since mid-2022. Reuters reported that Qatari mediators traveled to Tehran on Sunday as part of an effort to de-escalate regional tensions in coordination with the United States, and that Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir, and Turkey's intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin were set for regional security talks with Iranian officials on Monday. President Donald Trump said Sunday on Fox News' "Sunday Morning Futures" that he was weighing a deal with Tehran against further military action. The diplomacy has not stopped the attacks: the Houthis said they struck pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West crude pipeline and Riyadh's airport over the weekend, Saudi Arabia said Sunday it had intercepted more missiles and drones, and the kingdom's main airport only partly resumed operations on Sunday after damage from an earlier round of attacks. IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol said Monday he does not see energy markets returning to their prewar state in 2027. The incentive structure is the calendar: mediation before November 3 eases the fuel-price pressure on the incumbent's party, but Trump has not dispelled market expectations that strikes could follow the election, so traders priced Monday's pullback as position adjustment rather than a settlement — Vandana Hari of Vanda Insights said the reprieve may prove temporary and volatility would remain elevated with risks skewed upward.

OCT 22

/// The Drones Were Never in the Plans

On October 21, the Justice Department told a federal judge that construction on President Trump's proposed 250-foot triumphal arch at Memorial Circle could begin as early as November 5 — two days after the midterm elections — if the National Capital Planning Commission approves the final plans at its meeting that day and the Department of the Interior issues its authorization. The final plans, released the week before, describe the project as a "ceremonial gateway" topped by a golden winged statue holding an "eternal flame," and contain none of the military features Trump announced in September — no storage for large numbers of drones, no sniper posts, no ammunition cache. The arch would stand directly across from Arlington National Cemetery, where nearly 430,000 service members, veterans, and family members are buried. Veterans suing to block it argue the structure would intrude on military funerals; preservationists say it would break the sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and the cemetery. The incentive structure: the September announcement sold the project as a military complex, a justification that blunts opposition to a presidential monument, while the October filing — the version that must survive judges and planning review — carries none of it. The schedule puts the first legally possible construction date two days after the midterms.

OCT 22

/// The Rule That Died Without a Word

On October 21, the Justice Department dropped two appeals defending a rule that would have let the Education Secretary remove employers from the Public Service Loan Forgiveness program over a "substantial illegal purpose." The withdrawals were single-sentence joint agreements — each side drops the appeals and pays its own legal fees — and the administration offered no explanation. The rule, finalized in October 2025, would have let Secretary Linda McMahon disqualify employers based on her judgment of their activities; critics said the undefined standard targeted organizations that help undocumented immigrants or provide gender-affirming care, potentially affecting borrowers at schools, public hospitals, and legal-aid groups. It never took effect: on June 30, 2026, U.S. District Judge Myong Joun in Massachusetts ruled the rule contrary to law, arbitrary and capricious, and a First Amendment violation, and on the same day U.S. District Judge Amir Ali in Washington issued a similar ruling. The administration appealed in August. Wednesday's filings end two of the appeals; one appeal remains pending. PSLF has canceled the loans of more than 1 million public servants. The incentive structure: the program is too popular to attack directly, so the attack ran sideways — keep the forgiveness, let one appointee decide which employers count. The silent retreat comes less than two weeks before the midterms, with the administration offering no explanation.

OCT 22

/// The Referee Is on the Field

Reuters reported October 22 that the November 3 midterms arrive after an electoral cycle shaped "to an unusual degree" by Supreme Court decisions, with Republicans faring better than Democrats in most of the election-related rulings. The ledger: in April, the Court's conservative majority gutted a key provision of the Voting Rights Act, prompting Republican-led Southern states to redraw House districts with large Black populations; in June, the Court struck down federal caps on coordinated party-candidate campaign spending in a 6-3 decision on a challenge brought by Vice President JD Vance, the NRSC, and the NRCC — Republican committees have since exceeded the old limits by more than $48 million in July and August disclosures, with Democrats exceeding them by under $4 million; this month the Court also allowed a policy giving parties, not just candidates, discounted TV and radio ad rates. The Court let Texas's redrawn map and California's pro-Democratic map stand while barring Missouri's pro-Republican redraw, and last month rebuffed a Trump-backed bid to restrict mail voting with only Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito dissenting. Democrats need a net gain of four seats in each chamber to flip Congress. On October 21, Justice Elena Kagan told students at Catholic University's law school that the conservative majority has "created a race to the bottom" in district-drawing — "both political parties have been engaged in a race to the bottom... effectively depriving masses of citizens of their rights to voting equality" — and called the pattern "very destructive to our democracy." The incentive structure is the pipeline itself: parties invest in judges because rulings move elections without moving voters, and the Court's 2019 decision removing federal courts from policing partisan gerrymanders handed both sides a green light neither has stopped using. Polls still put Democrats in a strong position for a House majority — the rulings have tilted the field without sealing the outcome.

OCT 20

/// Tehran Stalls the Talks to Bleed Trump Before November 3

On October 20, Reuters reported that three senior Iranian officials and an Iranian insider said Tehran is deliberately prolonging talks with Washington to weaken President Donald Trump politically ahead of the November 3 midterm elections, convinced he is ready to resume attacks to break the stalemate in the eight-month-old war. "Iran is preparing for renewed Israeli and US attacks, not for a deal with America to end the war," one official said; "the clerical establishment is determined not to give Trump a political victory." Analyst Saeed Leylaz said Iran is "influencing the US election without taking any unlawful action," while Alex Vatanka of the Middle East Institute said the clerical establishment wants to hurt Trump politically and believes he seeks regime change. The reporting says the war, which began in February with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran, has driven Trump's approval rating to a record low as voters worried about gasoline prices prepare to punish his party. On October 8, Trump wrote on Truth Social that the United States would not attack Iran before the midterms, while keeping the naval blockade in full force and continuing "productive discussions." Iran's proposal, conveyed through Qatari mediators, offers to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, under Iranian control, in exchange for lifting the blockade, easing oil-export restrictions, releasing frozen assets, and ending hostilities on all fronts — an offer Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi said on October 18 remains on the table. On October 19, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth discussed Iran with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in an unannounced visit to Israel. The incentive structure: Tehran converts the pre-election window into pressure — a stalemate that keeps oil prices elevated costs the incumbent's party, while any concession before November 3 would hand Trump the political victory the officials say the clerical establishment refuses to give. Iran pays for the posture domestically: inflation hit 73.6% in September per the Statistical Centre of Iran, the rial has fallen nearly 35% against the dollar since Washington reapplied its naval blockade in July, and seaborne crude exports fell to zero in the second week of September from 230,000 barrels a day, according to Vortexa.

OCT 20

/// Vance Demotes Ukraine to "Partner" Over the Refinery Strikes

In a 2WAY TONIGHT interview reported October 20 by Ukrinform, Vice President JD Vance said Ukraine is a partner of the United States "in a certain sense" but should stop striking Russian oil refineries. "Do I think they should be striking Russian diesel fuel production facilities, which lead to rising energy prices for Americans? No," Vance said. Asked whether "partner" was weaker than "ally," he replied: "We don't have a formal alliance as far as I know with Ukraine. They're not in NATO. I don't think that they should be in NATO." The remarks land as the refinery war keeps biting: on October 16, two industry sources told Reuters that Russia's Ryazan refinery — one of the country's largest, with processing capacity of 17 million metric tons a year and diesel output of 3.8 million tons — halted one of its four primary crude distillation units, CDU-6, after the October 15 drone attack, and Russian state television broadcast refinery-attack aftermath footage for the first time in the war. Trump told Newsmax the refinery strikes are a "disgrace" that keeps global energy prices elevated. Reuters reported October 20 that diesel prices have hit record highs in many markets including the United States, where the fuel has become a talking point ahead of the November 3 midterms. The incentive split: Washington wants fuel prices down before the vote — the administration has pressed Kyiv to stop the strikes and warned of consequences — while Kyiv treats the refinery campaign as its bargaining chip over both Moscow's revenue and Washington's weapons decisions.

/// The IEA Front-Loads Diesel as 2027 Recedes From View

On October 20, IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol told an FT-organized conference in London that the agency stands ready to coordinate further emergency oil releases if member countries choose it: "If it is needed... and if our countries consider it's time to push the button, as the International Energy Agency, we are ready to go." The remarks follow the IEA board's October 15 decision to accelerate the release of remaining volumes from the record March collective action — pledged at more than 400 million barrels of crude and oil products — including over 20 million barrels of diesel to be delivered as soon as possible. Members have so far released 80% of the March commitment. Reuters reported October 15 that Trump has pressed EU countries to draw down their reserves or face a potential U.S. diesel export ban as he seeks lower prices before the November 3 midterms, and that the October 15 release is an acceleration of existing commitments rather than genuinely new volumes, per analysts at Sparta Commodities. Birol said the 20 million barrels of diesel are the priority and deliveries will be front-loaded: "We're trying to front-load those diesel deliveries." The market context: Brent rose above $111 a barrel on October 20, a five-month high, and European benchmark diesel futures traded at $1,482.25 per metric ton, up almost 4% since the accelerated-release announcement. Birol said global diesel markets could remain tight well into next year — "I personally don't see any credible pathway that 2027 will be a year of normalisation." The incentive structure: strategic reserves are a finite cushion being spent on a supply disruption — the Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine refinery campaign — that shows no sign of ending, while the political pressure on governments comes from diesel prices that hit trucking, agriculture, and industry directly.

OCT 20

/// Russian Diesel Steams Back to Turkey and Brazil

On October 20, Reuters reported that Russian diesel cargoes are returning to their previous main customers after the U.S.-brokered partial lifting of Moscow's diesel export ban. Shipping data from Kpler and LSEG show the tanker Leruo signaling Manaus, Brazil, after departing the Baltic port of Primorsk on October 16, following the Dolunay, which departed Primorsk with a diesel cargo on October 14 bound for Alanya, Turkey. Turkey and Brazil have been the two largest buyers of Russian diesel since 2023: Turkey has taken about 34% of Russian diesel loadings this year (95,000 barrels per day) and Brazil 24% (66,000 bpd), according to Kpler. Russia is normally the world's second-largest diesel exporter after the United States, but it cut exports in the summer and imposed the export ban as it grappled with widespread Ukrainian attacks on its refineries. On October 15, the Kremlin said diesel had begun reaching global markets under the deal with Trump — but the arithmetic is thin: Trump's figure of more than 300,000 metric tons (2.25 million barrels) for the immediate shipment is less than a single day's U.S. transport-sector demand, with the EIA showing daily use of about 2.94 million barrels; the promised 500,000 tons in November and 1 million tons after that cover less than four days' demand combined. The incentive loop: Moscow converts the export-ban lift back into revenue through its established Turkey and Brazil channels, while Washington gets a visible flow of fuel it can point to ahead of the November 3 midterms — one whose volume, per the data, is far smaller than the price relief it is meant to deliver.

OCT 19

/// Warsh Says Nothing, and Markets Hear Him

On October 16, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his fireside chat with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva in Bangkok — his last scheduled public appearance before the quiet period ahead of the October 27–28 FOMC meeting — to refuse any signal on the next rate decision. "I don't prejudge meetings. I don't believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of this year," Warsh said, adding that "the world has broadly conformed with our expectations" since September's quarter-point hike brought the target range to 3.75–4.00%. Warsh reaffirmed that the Fed had "made very clear that we would deliver price stability" after September CPI printed 3.7% year over year, well above the 2% target. On artificial intelligence, he called the investment surge "probably the most important thing that's happening in the global economy on the upside" — inflationary in the near term through demand for products like memory chips, disinflationary over time through productivity — while admitting policymakers do not know its effect on employment over the next 12 to 18 months. He also announced that his productivity-and-jobs task force will hold its first field hearing in the Bay Area on November 16. Traders read the remarks as trimming the odds of an October hike; CME FedWatch now prices a hold on October 28 and a quarter-point hike in December, and the September meeting minutes show most FOMC officials expect one more hike this year. The incentive structure is that silence moves the October decision — the last FOMC before the November 3 midterms — off the Fed's communications and onto the data, while the no-guidance regime Warsh is building keeps rate-path projections out of political crossfire. His colleagues, who have spent October arguing in more than 40 public appearances that communication is part of the job, remain the counterweight.

OCT 19

/// Trump Waives the Sanctions Law He Signed Last Month

On October 16, President Trump signed an executive order waiving key sanctions and tariffs under the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 — legislation he signed on September 18 after the Senate passed it 86–11 — two days before the measures were scheduled to take effect on October 18. The waived provisions included tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods, tariffs of up to 100% on the top five importers of Russian oil and gas — a group that includes China and India — and penalties aimed at Russia's "shadow fleet" tankers and its energy and defense sectors. On October 17, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump wanted up to 60 days to assess the economic impact of the measures: "These conflicts around the world are starting to crash into each other," he said, citing the war with Iran and Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia as forces lifting energy prices. Rubio argued the Russian diesel "will make it available to the global market one way or the other. The only difference is how much of it the United States was going to get." Trump himself told reporters at Mar-a-Lago that he had reached no final decision — "A lot of you don't know what my decision is yet, but we'll figure it out" — and wrote on Truth Social that his aim was avoiding anything "that will, foolishly, drive Russia away from the bargaining table." Zelenskyy called the waiver "wrong," saying it "definitely helps only the Russians." Republican Senators Thom Tillis ("beyond irresponsible") and Ted Cruz ("a mistake") joined the criticism. The documented incentives run in opposite directions: the administration's is fuel-price relief before the November 3 midterms — diesel is up roughly 70% on the year and Brent remains above $100 — while Congress's is the statutory leverage the Graham Act was designed to give Washington over Moscow, plus allied credibility in Ukraine.

OCT 19

/// Kyiv Strikes St. Petersburg While the Diesel Deal Stalls

Overnight from October 18 into 19, Ukraine launched one of the largest drone attacks of the war, with Russia's defense ministry saying it destroyed 1,015 Ukrainian drones across 15 regions. The heaviest damage was in St. Petersburg, where Governor Alexander Beglov said fires broke out at the Petersburg Oil Terminal and the Ruchyi oil depot, with one person killed and five hospitalized; an Ozon logistics center and a VK data center were also hit, flight operations were restricted at Pulkovo airport, and 12 housing blocks were damaged. The strike landed the same weekend the U.S.-proposed "energy ceasefire" visibly collapsed: on October 18, Zelenskyy wrote on X that in the week since Trump announced the truce, Russia had "deliberately attacked Ukraine's energy and oil and gas sectors 76 times," with the latest strikes hitting substations in Vinnytsia, Zhytomyr, Khmelnytskyi, and Odesa, leaving tens of thousands without power. That same Sunday, Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said the Russian diesel promised under the U.S. arrangement needs 15 to 20 days to reach American markets after commercial contracts are signed — and those contracts remain unsigned, with negotiations on commercial terms still underway. Novak put the schedule at 500,000 tons in October, one million tons in November, and one million tons in December, saying Russia could supply 3.5 million tons to world markets by year-end — nearly 10% of the global market — if refineries run at full capacity. The United States consumes about 3.9 million barrels of diesel a day. The incentive loop: Trump's pre-midterm fuel-price relief depends on Russian refinery capacity surviving the energy war, while Ukraine's strikes on that capacity are the leverage Kyiv is using to demand U.S. Patriot interceptors as its price for stopping them.

OCT 19

/// China's 4.5% Print Runs on Exports

China's National Bureau of Statistics reported October 19 that GDP grew 4.5% year on year in the third quarter, beating the 4.4% Reuters poll forecast and improving on the second quarter's 4.3%, a three-year low. Quarter-on-quarter growth was 1.2% against a 1.1% forecast. September industrial output rose 5.5%, retail sales grew 1.1% against a 0.6% forecast, and the urban jobless rate eased to 5.2%. Growth for the first nine months reached 4.6%, inside Beijing's 4.5–5% annual target. But the NBS cautioned that "insufficient domestic demand remains prominent": fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% over the nine-month period and property investment plunged 20.6%. Exports carried the print — chip exports more than doubled in value over the nine months, high-tech manufacturing output rose 14.3%, and the trade surplus reached $938.1 billion, keeping China on track to surpass 2025's record $1.2 trillion. The yuan firmed to 6.6828, its strongest level since July 2022. Earlier this month, the IMF cut its 2026 China forecast to 4.5%, warning that strong exports could not fully offset weak domestic demand. The incentive structure: an on-target number reduces Beijing's pressure to launch broad stimulus, letting policymakers keep redirecting credit from the property sector into high-tech industry while households sit it out. The counterpart risk is external — the widening surplus deepens trade friction with the EU and the U.S., and the EU is already moving to rein in Chinese hybrid-vehicle shipments.

OCT 15

/// Tariffs Rerouted the Trade

In its October World Economic Outlook, released October 13 at the IMF-World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok, the International Monetary Fund documented that the 2025 U.S.-China tariff increases moved trade to new routes while leaving total volumes near their pre-tariff paths. Chinese exports to the United States fell 22% and American exports to China fell 25% between February 2025 and January 2026, subtracting 3.3 percentage points from China's total export growth and 1.7 points from America's — yet both countries' total exports stayed within roughly one percentage point of their pre-tariff trajectories. For products hit by U.S. tariffs, Chinese exports to alternative destinations rose by an amount equal to 52% of the trade at risk within a year (39% after adjusting for expected growth); the U.S. equivalents were 17% and 7%. Asian economies absorbed roughly half of all redirected trade, with the European Union capturing close to one-fifth. The Fund projects world trade volume growth slowing from 5% in 2025 to 4.6% in 2026, recovering to 4.7% in 2027, and warns that persistent restrictions risk raising costs and reducing supply-chain efficiency over the longer term. Chief economist Silvana Tenreyro put the verdict in one line: "The global economy has been resilient, but that resilience is reaching its limits." The documented incentive runs through the exporting firms, which preserved sales volumes by finding new buyers — China, more exposed with 15% of its exports going to the U.S. against China's 7% share of U.S. exports, moved faster — and through third-country hubs positioning as intermediary platforms, with consumers absorbing the price of less-efficient routes.

OCT 15

/// The Gulf Faces a 2026 Contraction

The October World Economic Outlook also forecasts the Middle East and Central Asia economy will contract 0.3% in 2026 — a 2.2-percentage-point downgrade from the 1.9% expansion projected in April — on the Fund's assumption of a longer Strait of Hormuz closure than previously anticipated, with a 6.3% rebound expected in 2027 once the shipping route reopens. The country figures are the sharpest the Fund published in the round: Kuwait −16.7%, Iraq −16%, Qatar −15.6%, Bahrain −11.3%, Iran −7.6%, and Saudi Arabia −3.3% — this is the only region the IMF expects to contract in 2026, and the same double-digit contractions are forecast to flip to double-digit expansions in 2027 as production, exports and logistics normalize (Kuwait +17.1%, Qatar +18.8%, Iraq +19%, Bahrain +13.9%). Saudi Arabia fares comparatively better because it has used its East-West pipeline to move oil toward the Red Sea, reducing dependence on Hormuz for some exports. The closure is tightening in real time: the Singapore-registered container ship GFS Pride was struck on Tuesday, caught fire and sank — all crew evacuated — and the Antigua and Barbuda-registered Cape Dao sank on Wednesday after being hit September 23, killing one Indian crew member; the two are only the second and third commercial vessels sunk in the Middle East since the war began in February. UKMTO reported nine attacks in or near the strait since Monday, and only 4.147 million barrels of oil transited Hormuz on October 12 — less than a quarter of pre-war levels, with the seven-day average dropping sharply since early October. The IMF cites disrupted energy exports, higher shipping and insurance costs, and weakened investor confidence as the transmission channels. The divergence documents the incentive: export-route redundancy is institutional insurance that Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain lack — and the 2027 rebound rests on Hormuz reopening, a political variable rather than an economic one.

OCT 15

/// Kyiv Says the Refinery-Strike Pause Is Over

On the nights of October 13 and 14, Russia launched 39 ballistic missiles and more than 120 drones at Ukrainian energy infrastructure around Kyiv and Kharkiv, striking Kyiv's CHP-4 power plant on Tuesday evening among other targets; President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the strikes damaged at least 26 power facilities and four oil and gas sites and cut power to at least 600,000 people. Russia's defense ministry said it struck seven Ukrainian energy facilities and a data center. In an interview with the Financial Times published October 15, Zelenskyy said Ukraine would resume strikes on Russian refineries — "The block [on attacking refineries] is out. We're done" — saying Kyiv had paused after Washington announced its "energy ceasefire" initiative while Russian attacks continued. The ceasefire was announced by President Trump over the weekend; Zelenskyy denied ever agreeing to it, the Kremlin did not confirm it, and a White House official told Axios the proposal covered only refineries, "refinery for refinery." The U.S. pressure campaign is documented: the Trump administration warned Kyiv and European allies it could suspend intelligence sharing and European purchases of U.S. weapons for Ukraine if the strikes did not stop; Ukrainian officials were told by U.S. counterparts that "if you will not stop [the energy strikes], we will block intelligence" — later described to Kyiv as a technical glitch — and Zelenskyy said his forces received no early warnings for the incoming missiles while Starlink's military-grade service was slowed. On October 10, Trump had blamed Zelenskyy for high U.S. diesel prices — "wants to make problems for our farmers, our ranchers with diesel fuel" — and said "I suggest they get a new leader who can make a deal," one day after announcing the diesel-supply arrangement with Moscow. The same day the FT interview appeared, Ryazan's regional governor confirmed a Ukrainian drone attack caused a fire at the Rosneft-owned Ryazan refinery, about 300 km southeast of Moscow. The incentives are layered: Russia strikes generation capacity ahead of winter; Trump wants diesel prices down before the November 3 midterms, and the refinery strikes — which the International Energy Agency estimates have cut Russian diesel output by about 30% — work against that; Zelenskyy trades the strikes for fuel-revenue denial and names Patriot interceptors, "minimum," as his price for stopping again.

OCT 15

/// The Houthis Strike Aramco's Headquarters

On the night of October 13, Houthi forces launched missile and drone attacks across Saudi Arabia, and an administrative building at Saudi Aramco's Dhahran headquarters complex was hit. Saudi Arabia's energy ministry said the strike caused a "limited fire" and no injuries. Three people with direct knowledge told Reuters a different picture: the fire started on the ninth floor of the south wing of the office block and spread across the sixth to tenth floors; the top three floors, where chief executive Amin Nasser's office is located, were severely damaged, with ceilings caved in and windows and doors blown out. Operations at the complex continued, but some employees were told to work from home and some functions were shifted to the company's global offices. The facility houses the operations center through which the world's largest oil company oversees onshore and offshore oil rigs, refineries, export terminals and thousands of kilometers of pipeline. The attack came as fighting between the Iran-allied Houthis and the kingdom intensified: the strikes continued into October 14, at least 18 people have been killed and hundreds injured in Saudi Arabia over the past week, Riyadh's international airport has been hit four times, and the Saudi-led coalition launched a new Yemen offensive aimed at Houthi-held land near the Bab el-Mandeb strait, striking the Al Sabah oil facilities in Sanaa and targets in Saada and Al Jouf in return. The mechanism is a cost-escalation bid: the Houthis strike the administrative nerve center of the kingdom's oil economy to raise the price of Riyadh's new Yemen offensive, while the government's "limited fire" framing protects Aramco's operational credibility and the valuation built on it.

OCT 15

/// Inflation Broadens While the Fed Goes Quiet

On October 15, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the Producer Price Index for final demand jumped 1.3% in September — the largest monthly increase since March 2022, against a 0.5% consensus forecast — and rose 6.3% over twelve months, the largest since December 2022. Energy prices surged 10.8% and accounted for more than 80% of the broad-based rise in goods prices, with gasoline up 20.3%; services prices rose 0.8%, led by a 1.4% jump in trade-services margins. The same morning, the Census Bureau reported September retail sales up 0.8% against a 0.3% consensus — but excluding autos and gasoline the gain was only 0.2%, and higher gasoline prices drove most of the nominal increase; the Federal Reserve's Beige Book, released October 14, found most districts reporting "little or no change in overall consumer spending." The data land on a divided Federal Reserve: Reuters reported October 15 that Chair Kevin Warsh's push for a "quieter" Fed — no forward guidance, a task force expected to recommend dropping the quarterly rate-path projections — is meeting open resistance from colleagues who have made more than 40 public appearances arguing that communication is part of the job, and whose remarks convinced traders to scale back the rate-hike bets that mounted after September's increase. Wednesday's consumer-price report, 3.7% year over year, made an October hike a coin toss, and Warsh's October 16 appearance in Bangkok is the last scheduled Fed voice before the quiet period ahead of the October 27–28 meeting — six days before the November 3 midterms. Warsh's documented rationale is that silence forces markets to take their cues from economic data; his colleagues' is that communication is part of good monetary policy. The October 27–28 decision lands six days before the November 3 midterms, with inflation polling as voters' most urgent issue.

OCT 13

/// The Pre-Dawn Search of a Sitting Congresswoman

On Monday, October 12, agents with Homeland Security Investigations executed a judicially authorized search warrant at the Washington, D.C. home of Rep. Ilhan Omar (D-Minn.), the Department of Justice told NPR, describing it as part of an ongoing federal criminal investigation. Federal officials who spoke on background said the search is part of a multi-year probe involving whistleblower complaints; a former official said it concerns immigration fraud allegations. A neighbor told NPR agents arrived around 4:30 a.m. Senior DOJ leadership, including Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter, consulted on and approved the search. Omar — a naturalized U.S. citizen who came to the U.S. as a refugee from Somalia and is seeking reelection November 3 — said the search was "an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement" and that she would "not be silenced." Vice President JD Vance has previously alleged Omar committed immigration fraud, which she denies. No charges have been filed. The enforcement action lands in an institutional bind for the Justice Department: searching a sitting congresswoman three weeks before the midterms invites accusations of election-timed enforcement, while holding back invites accusations of favoritism — which is why DOJ's public posture rests on the warrant's judicial authorization and the multi-year, whistleblower-complaint basis of the probe. The documented facts establish a search, not guilt.

OCT 13

/// $20 Million of Your Money for Campaign-Style Ads

On the night of Sunday, October 11, U.S. District Judge Jennifer L. Rochon in New York granted a two-week temporary restraining order requiring the Trump administration to cancel any remaining airings of television ads paid for with taxpayer money to promote the president's political message, and barred the administration from entering into, renewing, or expanding any agreement to develop, create, place, or air the ads. The Department of Homeland Security allocated $20 million — drawn through U.S. Customs and Border Protection — and a sworn DHS declaration said ad agency LMD Agency spent about $19 million on media buys through October 11. The plaintiffs, a coalition led by the watchdog group Common Cause with labor unions and an Alabama Democratic state senate candidate, argued the ads violate the federal ban on using congressionally appropriated funds for "publicity or propaganda," and Rochon found them likely to succeed. The Justice Department argued the order was unnecessary because the buy would end October 11; Rochon rejected that, noting the contract with LMD Agency runs through September 2027 and the government offered no guarantee the ads would not resume. The White House said Trump's political action committee, MAGA PAC, would pay for the ads going forward but would not reimburse the public money already spent. A DHS declaration said the funds were appropriated for, among other things, "commemorating efforts and events related to border security," and Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the ads "shouldn't be paid for with taxpayer dollars." The material incentive is direct: $20 million in appropriated border-security funds reached voters as campaign-style advertising — including during Sunday football — without the campaign paying for it, three weeks before the November 3 midterms. The plaintiffs' institutional incentive runs the other way: establish a precedent that no president can redirect appropriated agency money into political promotion.

OCT 13

/// Moscow Diesel, Three Weeks Before the Midterms

On Monday, October 12, President Trump defended a diesel deal struck after his October 9 phone call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, in which Russia agreed to supply more than 300,000 metric tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tons in November, and one million tons shortly after — plus a conditional three million tons depending on Russian refinery capacity. The same day as the call, Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued General License 135, authorizing specified transactions in Russian-origin diesel, including imports into the United States, through 12:01 a.m. EDT on April 7, 2027. Reuters reported diesel prices have surged more than 60% since the U.S. began its war against Iran seven months ago, and high fuel prices have jeopardized Republican chances of holding thin congressional majorities in the November 3 midterms. On October 11, a bipartisan group of senators urged Trump to "reverse course," arguing the deal may violate a 2022 law barring Russian energy imports until Moscow ends its aggression against Ukraine; Republican Senator Jerry Moran said "we should be tightening sanctions further, not undercutting them." Trump said the deal would help American farmers and ranchers, blamed Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries as a leading factor in higher prices, and declared that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to halt attacks on each other's energy infrastructure — a claim the Kremlin described only as contacts "proceeding." Analysts cited by Reuters doubted the pledged volumes — equal to about 12 hours of global diesel consumption over three months — would move prices. The material incentive is pre-midterm fuel-price relief: diesel is the working fuel of agriculture, trucking, and home heating, and the license runs through April 2027. The counter-pressure is institutional: senators from both parties defending the statutory sanctions framework and allied credibility, in a live legal fight over whether a Treasury general license can sit under a congressional import ban.

OCT 13

/// Treasury Yields Hit 24-Year Highs

The 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.365% and the 30-year 5.733% last week — the highest levels in 24 years — before easing to 5.199% and 5.557% in Tuesday's Asian session. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller told a Bloomberg event in Mumbai on Tuesday that the rise reflects stronger economic growth, not inflation expectations, which remain "well anchored": "all of the build-up (in yields) is not coming from an inflation premium... it is a factor of real growth." Waller cited academic research suggesting U.S. government debt has lost some of its global safe-haven status over the past two years, reducing demand and potentially adding 50 to 70 basis points to yields, and pointed to AI-related investment — including data-center construction — supporting demand for another two to three years. He downplayed the Fed's own role, saying changes in the overnight cost of money are "not driving the 10-year yield much." Markets currently price a 19% probability of a Fed rate hike at the October 27–28 meeting, and analysts surveyed by the Wall Street Journal expect Wednesday's September inflation data to show 3.6% year-over-year, up from 3.4% in August. The institutional incentive is in the framing: the Fed raised rates September 16 to 3.75–4.00%, and Waller's public account places the drivers of higher long-term borrowing costs — growth, AI capex, eroded safe-haven demand — outside the Fed's responsibility. For the Treasury, the material fact is simpler: the government must refinance a $1.993 trillion fiscal-2026 deficit at the highest long-term yields in a generation.

OCT 13

/// Beijing Sanctions Two Ministers Over Taiwan Ties

On Monday, October 12, China's foreign ministry announced sanctions on Paraguay's Minister of Information and Communication Technologies Gustavo Villate and Belize's Foreign Minister Francis Fonseca: barring them from mainland China, Hong Kong, and Macau; freezing any assets they hold in China; and prohibiting Chinese organizations and individuals from transactions or cooperation with them. Foreign ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said the two were "anti-China" and "willing to serve as pawns" for Taiwan in keeping their countries' diplomatic recognition of Taipei — Fonseca met Taiwan's foreign minister in Belize in September, and Villate met Taiwan's deputy foreign minister in Paraguay in June. Paraguay and Belize are two of only 12 countries that maintain formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan. Reuters noted the sanctions are largely symbolic — officials from Taiwan's diplomatic allies generally do not travel to China — and that Beijing has previously applied such measures mainly to Western politicians backing Taiwan. On Tuesday, State Department spokesperson Tommy Pigott said the Trump administration opposes "coercion and aggression" by China against countries "in our hemisphere" exercising their sovereign rights, citing Secretary of State Marco Rubio's position that the U.S. "will not allow our region to become a base of operation for adversaries." The mechanism is cheap deterrence: raise the personal cost for officials of Taiwan's remaining allies and warn the other ten against following suit, preserving Beijing's institutional claim to Taiwan. The U.S. response's institutional incentive is keeping that diplomatic network alive and asserting primacy in Latin America — with neither side imposing costs that carry real economic weight.

OCT 12

/// The Jobs Report That Killed the October Hike

On October 2, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls rose 29,000 in September — against roughly 84,000 expected in a Dow Jones survey of economists — while the unemployment rate ticked to 4.2% and average hourly earnings rose 0.1% to $37.81, up 3.0% over the year. The revisions cut deeper than the miss: July was revised from +21,000 to −10,000 and August from 162,000 to 133,000, erasing 60,000 jobs from the summer record. The print landed two weeks after the Federal Reserve's September 16 decision, under Chair Kevin Warsh, to raise the federal funds rate a quarter point to 3.75–4.00% in a unanimous 12–0 vote — the first hike since July 2023, with the September statement citing job gains "keeping pace with the workforce." The September meeting minutes, released October 7, showed most participants expecting another hike by year end, but the weak report flipped market pricing: CNBC reported that CME FedWatch put the probability of a hold at the October 27–28 meeting at 82.8% after the release. New York Fed President John Williams said September 29 there was "no need for urgency" on further moves; Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said October 1 the committee's judgment "may take more time." The sequence documents the mechanism: the labor-market case behind the September hike rested on data that was revised away two weeks later, and the institutional cost of hiking again before the December 8–9 meeting now runs into visibly softening payrolls.

OCT 12

/// The $30 Billion Refund Chase

On October 8, Court of International Trade Judge Richard K. Eaton certified an injunctive class action in Freestyle World, Inc. v. United States (Slip Op. 26-123), covering importers of record that paid duties under the IEEPA tariff orders between February 1, 2025 and February 19, 2026 and have not filed their own suits — "some tens of thousands of importers," drawn from roughly 330,000 importers of record that paid the duties. The class seeks a single court order directing Customs and Border Protection to liquidate or reliquidate — re-open and re-process — covered entries without the IEEPA duties; the ruling orders no refunds yet. It exists because CBP's CAPE refund portal, which has returned more than $100 billion, does not accept finally liquidated entries (closed more than about 80 days), entries under protest, or non-ACE entries — and Eaton wrote that the importers left out are "overwhelmingly small importers" who will never file because of the cost and effort, leaving an estimated $30 billion in unlawful duties with the government absent a class order. The duties were collected under IEEPA orders the Supreme Court struck down 6–3 on February 20, 2026; the administration replaced them with a 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a never-before-used authority capped at 15% for 150 days — with Trump announcing an increase to 15%, while the Section 232 and Section 301 tariffs were never challenged and remain in place. The incentives run both ways: importers' material interest is recovering duties the courts say were never lawfully owed; the administration's is preserving tariff revenue and negotiating leverage — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNN the Section 122 tariffs are a temporary bridge while Section 232 and 301 investigations are completed, and U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer is preparing Section 301 actions to rebuild the prior tariff levels.

OCT 12

/// Tokyo Bows to the Bond Market

On October 5, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told an extraordinary Diet session that her government would "control" bond issuance and "act swiftly" against market turbulence, calling fiscal sustainability "obviously a prerequisite" for what she termed "responsible and proactive" fiscal policy — including a review of existing tax breaks and subsidies. She spoke the same day the 30-year Japanese government bond yield hit a record 4.235%, driven by expectations that her spending plans would flood the bond market and by inflation fears from the Middle East war; Japan's public debt stands near twice GDP, the heaviest in the developed world, while the Bank of Japan is raising rates and tapering bond purchases. On October 8, Reuters reported her pledge to cap new debt issuance near ¥40 trillion (about $253 billion) against record ¥143 trillion in ministry spending requests for next year's budget, alongside a planned two-year food sales-tax cut costing roughly ¥4 trillion a year — to be funded, she says, from rising tax revenues and state funds, not new bonds. The yen slid past ¥158 to the dollar this week, its fourth straight weekly decline; SBI Securities bond strategist Eiji Doke told Reuters that markets "look at the substance of policy and won't believe words alone." The mechanism is visible in the numbers: a government that came to power pledging fiscal expansion needs its creditors' permission to keep borrowing cheap, and the bond market is pricing the gap between the ¥40 trillion cap and the ¥143 trillion in requests.

OCT 12

/// The €1.8 Trillion Standoff

On October 10, Ireland's presidency of the Council of the EU circulated its revised "negotiating box" for the 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework: about €1.62 trillion in 2025 prices (€1.82–1.83 trillion in current prices), roughly 8% — €141 to €159 billion — below the European Commission's nearly €2 trillion proposal. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz rejected it the same day as "not an acceptable basis for negotiations," insisting the budget "must be sustainable for those who provide the bulk of [its] funding" — joined by Sweden, the Netherlands, Finland, Austria and Denmark, six "frugal" states that together finance nearly 40% of EU revenues; Austria's Europe minister demanded "several hundred billion euros" in further cuts, and the Dutch finance minister said no deal this year is likely at this rate. The cuts are uneven: farm and cohesion spending falls about 3% to €914 billion, while competitiveness and security programs take a 13% cut (to €456 billion) and external action 17%; the plan also adds about €55 billion a year in new own resources from customs duties, carbon-permit auction revenues and a levy on large companies. A European Parliament that wants 10% more than the Commission proposed is pushing back too, with its environment committee meeting October 12 to warn the cuts would gut climate funding; France, the bloc's second-largest economy, says Europe needs the means to match its challenges. EU leaders take up the plan at the October 15–16 European Council; the seven-year budget needs unanimous approval from all 27 states, and Ireland wants a political deal by Christmas — before a 2027 election year in France, Italy, Spain and Poland that leaders fear could empower eurosceptic parties and freeze the talks.

OCT 10

/// The $2 Trillion Deficit

The Congressional Budget Office put the fiscal 2026 deficit at $1.993 trillion — up $218 billion (12%) from 2025, the largest since 2021. Spending hit $7.4 trillion (+6%); revenue $5.4 trillion (+3%). That's a wartime-sized gap, over 6% of GDP, during an economic expansion with low unemployment — the kind of deficit economists expect from recessions, not growth years. The administration cut the federal workforce and clean-energy breaks; the deficit widened anyway, driven by tariff refunds, immigration-enforcement spending, extended tax cuts, and entitlements.

OCT 10

/// France's Bond Buyers Revolt

On October 1, France's 10-year bond yield touched 5 percent — highest since July 2002 — with the premium over German bonds widening to about 1.5 points, a gap not seen since the 2011 eurozone debt crisis. The trigger: a minority government's 2027 budget plan (€54 billion in cuts and tax measures) against public debt near 120% of GDP and record €340 billion in planned borrowing. In March 1983, Mitterrand reversed his socialist program under currency-market pressure; this time the pressure comes from bond buyers, with France's annual interest bill projected to rise from €62.6 billion to €72.9 billion.

Anduril's founder Palmer Luckey has given about $5.9 million to Republican causes since 2017 and was named a Pentagon adviser by Defense Secretary Hegseth the week before the award. The shipyard won't produce parts until 2030.
