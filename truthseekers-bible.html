The Truthseeker's Bible

Twelve rules for reading the news without getting played — plus a jargon decoder that translates what politicians and pundits actually mean. The game is rigged to keep you reacting instead of thinking. These are the rules for opting out.

By Jacob · Editor · A living document — corrected in public, never quietly.

Why this exists

Nobody handed you this in school. The news doesn't have to lie to you to steer you — it just has to pick your frame, pick your villain, and keep you too angry to check the math. These rules are the antidote. They're not left or right. They work on every outlet, every party, and us too. If you ever catch Distortion Daily breaking one of these rules, that's a correction, and corrections go on the front page.

The twelve rules

1. Follow the incentives. Before you believe a claim, ask who profits if you believe it. A politician, a pundit, and a lobbyist can all say true things — but they only say the true things that pay. Find the paycheck behind the position and half the mystery evaporates.

2. Steelman first. State the other side's best argument fairly before you swing at it. If you can only beat the dumb version of their case, you haven't beaten their case. This rule alone would defuse most internet arguments.

3. Separate the claim from the claimant. A true fact from a liar is still true. A lie from your hero is still a lie. Check the claim on its own — the evidence, the source material, who else confirms it. A fact doesn't turn false because you dislike who said it, and a lie doesn't turn true because you like who said it.

4. Demand the receipt. "Studies show," "experts say," "everyone knows" — with no study, no expert, no numbers — is a rumor in a suit. Ask: which study, how many people, who paid for it, what did it actually find? No receipt, no belief.

5. Spot the frame. The same fact can be framed two opposite ways, and both can be technically true. "Unemployment down" and "wages still flat" can describe the same month. Always ask what's being left out of the picture — the crop is the message.

6. Run the tribe test. Take an action your side did and imagine the other side doing it — or the reverse. If it's wrong, it's wrong regardless of whose jersey is on. If your verdict flips when the team flips, the verdict was about the team.

7. Anecdote isn't data. Your uncle's story, one viral video, a single tragedy — these may be real, and they may matter deeply. But one case can't tell you how common something is. And the other side has no incentive to take your story on faith — without receipts, even a true story convinces nobody. Feelings tell you it hurts. Numbers tell you how often. Bring both.

8. Correlation isn't causation. Two things rising together doesn't mean one caused the other. Ask what else changed at the same time, and ask which direction the arrow points — sometimes the "effect" was already underway before the "cause" showed up.

9. If nothing could prove it wrong, it's a slogan. A real claim sticks its neck out: it tells you what evidence would change its mind. If no possible fact could shake it — if every outcome somehow "proves" it right — then it was never built to be tested. Treat it as a chant: repeatable, untestable, useless for finding out what's true.

10. Check the date, then check the source. Old footage recirculates as breaking news every single week. And a pundit summarizing a study is not the study — go one click upstream to the primary source whenever you can. Whoever is furthest from the raw facts has the most room to spin.

11. Your anger is the product. If a headline wants you furious before you've finished the first paragraph, check the business model: outrage keeps you scrolling, and scrolling sells ads. Notice the hook, then read past it on purpose.

12. Admit what you don't know. Most of the time, "I don't know yet" is the only honest answer. Anyone selling you total certainty about a messy, unfolding story is selling something. The strongest sentence in politics is "I don't know yet — here's what would convince me."

★ Why it works on you

Here's the part nobody tells you: manipulation doesn't hack your logic. It hacks your upbringing. The wounds from before you could consent — the shouting, the absence, the lessons beaten in — leave triggers under the floorboards. A message that rhymes with an old wound bypasses your thinking and goes straight for the feeling. It lands because it feels familiar — recognition disguised as agreement. That's why two people can read the same lie and only one of them bleeds.

So ask the uncomfortable question: which old chord is this message tuned to strike? The fear chord. The shame chord. The belonging chord — the terror of being cast out of the group, which is how the tribe collects so many minds. Name the chord and the spell weakens. The people writing the message know exactly which strings they're pulling. Learn the instrument and their grip loosens. Your mind is public domain — no legal protection. Act like somebody's already writing on it.

One more distinction that changes everything: the installed self vs. the chosen self. A lot of what you call "your opinions" was installed before you were old enough to consent — by parents, preachers, schools, feeds. None of that is your fault. But it is your inventory to take. The work is simple and brutal: sort your beliefs into the ones you chose and the ones that were installed. Nobody gets mocked here for running installed software. The people who installed it are fair game.

★ How they steer you

Once you know why you're vulnerable, here's how it gets exploited. Standard playbook, no conspiracy required:

They tell you what to think about. The news can't easily tell you what to think — but it absolutely decides what you think about. When ten outlets lead with the same story for a week, that's agenda-setting in action. Ask: what isn't leading tonight, and who benefits from that silence?

Someone always decides what you never see. Every feed, every front page, every "trending" list has a gatekeeper — an editor, an algorithm, or both. What reaches you is what survived their filter. Ask: who built this gate, and what do they get paid for?

The frame is the message. Same facts, different packaging, different gut reaction: "tax relief" vs. "tax cuts for the rich." "Protest" vs. "riot." "Reform" vs. "power grab." Both versions can be technically true. The packaging tells you which conclusion they pre-ordered for you. (See Rule 5.)

Outrage is the distribution strategy. Platforms don't spread what's true — they spread what grabs. Anger and novelty travel fastest, so the most enraging version of every story outruns the accurate one. Virality is not credibility. (See Rule 11.)

★ The jargon decoder

Power hides behind vocabulary. Here's what the words actually mean:

  • "Leverage" — a threat. When they won't say threat, ask why.
  • "Concessions" — the other side gave something up. Ask what, specifically, or it's just a word.
  • "Strategic ambiguity" — we won't tell you the plan. Sometimes that's wisdom; usually it's cover.
  • "Quantitative easing" — creating money. Say it plain and suddenly everyone has opinions.
  • "Collateral damage" — dead civilians. The jargon exists so you don't picture them.
  • "Enhanced interrogation" — torture. Renamed by the people doing it.
  • "Rightsizing" / "streamlining" — layoffs. Your job, dissolved into a synonym.
  • "Job creators" — rich people, framed so tax cuts for them sound like charity for you.
  • "Entitlements" — benefits you paid into, reframed as handouts. Social Security isn't a gift.
  • "Pro-growth" — good for GDP numbers. GDP doesn't buy groceries; ask who grows.
  • "Bipartisan" — both teams agreed. Sometimes that's statesmanship; sometimes they just agreed on who gets billed. Check.
  • "Thoughts and prayers" (as policy) — we will do nothing, but we wanted credit for caring.
  • "National security" (as a dodge) — we won't answer the question. Occasionally legitimate, mostly a curtain. A real secret has a shape; a dodge is just fog.

Rule zero

This bible can be wrong. If a rule misfires, if a translation is unfair, if a better rule exists — that's a correction, and it goes on the front page like any other. A field manual that can't be corrected is a catechism. We're not building one of those.

Verdict: nobody is coming to deprogram the news for you. The frame-pickers count on you never learning the tricks — because the tricks only work on people who don't know they're tricks. Now you know them. Use them on everyone, including us.
