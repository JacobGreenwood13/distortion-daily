The 100% Tariff Bluff: Everybody's Playing Poker With Your Wallet

Washington's got a loaded tariff cannon — up to 100% duties aimed at the biggest buyers of Russian oil, China and India first in line — and nobody's pulled the trigger. That unfired gun is the whole strategy. Here's who's supposed to flinch, who gets the bill if it fires, and the story they're telling you while you foot the bill.

By Jacob · Editor · September 20, 2026 · 6 min read

The facts

Strip out the noise and here's what's on the table. On September 18, 2026, the White House signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act (H.R. 5334), which gives the president authority to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the five largest buyers of Russian oil and gas — China and India are the obvious targets. The authority hasn't been used. That's the tell: the threat is the weapon. Cut your Russian energy purchases, or the duties go live.

Now the single fact the whole debate orbits: a tariff is a tax on imports, and the importer pays it. The American company bringing the goods through the dock pays the tax to the U.S. government — then passes most of the cost down the line until it lands on you, at the register. China doesn't get the invoice. India doesn't get the invoice. (Some gets absorbed by importers and retailers along the way — but study after study of the last tariff rounds lands in the same place: the bill fell overwhelmingly on Americans, not on foreign exporters.) The invoice doesn't care about your politics.

So the board comes down to two poles. The threat works and never fires — nobody pays anything, and Washington claims a win. Or the threat fires — and American shoppers and businesses eat a 100% tax on some of the cheapest goods in the economy, while the targeted countries hit back at American exporters. Reality will land somewhere between, but those are the poles. Everything else is commentary.

What the hawks claim

Maximum pressure works. China only respects strength, the threat squeezes out concessions, American industry finally gets its shield — and foreigners foot the bill. Free money for the treasury, costs you nothing.

The first three are arguable. The last one is arithmetic with a flag pin: foreigners don't pay the tariff. The importer pays it, then you pay the importer. We'll keep the receipts on that one.

What the doves claim

This is a tax on Americans in a tough-guy costume. Importers pay it and hand you the bill. Beijing retaliates against our exporters. The working stiff gets squeezed from both ends while politicians mug for the cameras back home.

On the economics, the receipts back them. On strategy, they owe an answer the hawks never have to give: what makes Beijing move without leverage? Scolding without leverage is losing with good manners.

Why it matters to you

Forget the cable-news chessboard for a second. Here's the version that hits your actual life: if that cannon fires, the price of everyday goods — clothes, electronics, the stuff that fills a dollar store — jumps, because the tax gets paid at the dock and collected at your register. Not in theory. At the checkout, every week, out of the same paycheck that already doesn't stretch.

And if the cannon never fires? Then the "win" is a press release about concessions you'll never see itemized, negotiated behind doors you'll never enter. Either way, you're the only player at the table who doesn't get a guaranteed payoff. Remember that the next time someone tells you this is being done for you. It's done with your money, at your expense — the "for you" is the costume they wear for the cameras.

★ Follow the incentives

Everyone at this table is acting rationally. That's the scandal — nobody has to be corrupt for you to lose. The machine is working exactly as designed. You're just not the one it was designed for. Follow what each player gets paid in.

  • The White House gets the tough-guy headlines at zero cost. "Tough on China" polls great; doubled prices at the store poll like a funeral. So the rational move is threaten loud, fire never, and let the base feel protected by a gun that stays in the holster. The catch: if Beijing calls and Washington blinks, the bluff is spent — so the threat has to keep escalating to stay credible. That's the ratchet, and it only turns one way.
  • Beijing and Delhi get free theater at wholesale prices. Their cheapest move is to toss Washington just enough cooperation for a victory lap — a purchase pledge, a summit handshake — and surrender nothing structural. And if the tariff ever does fire, they retaliate against American exporters, because pain aimed at voters gets repealed faster than pain aimed at politicians.
  • Congress gets to dodge the vote. Tariffs-by-threat mean no recorded vote on a tax hike — members can cheer the toughness or boo the recklessness without a single fingerprint on the policy. Posture without ownership is every politician's favorite position.
  • The carve-out racket gets the real money. The moment duties look real, every industry hires lobbyists to beg for exemptions — and exemptions are where fortunes get made. The connected get carved out; the unconnected pay full freight. The threat doesn't just pressure Beijing. It shakes down K Street — the D.C. strip where lobbyists sell access.
  • The pundit class gets the fight itself. Hawks fundraise off the menace, doves fundraise off the warning, cable gets a segment. Nobody's paycheck depends on a scoreboard ever being published — so nobody asks for one.
  • You get the bill if it fires — at the register, every week — and a press release if it doesn't. You're the only player whose payoff isn't guaranteed either way. Everybody's playing poker with your wallet and you're not even at the table.

What a serious version looks like

Assume the stated goal is genuine — real trade concessions, real protection for American workers. Fine. Here's what that policy would include, and you should notice that none of it exists right now:

  • A public scoreboard. Name the specific concessions being demanded — measurable, dated, verifiable. If the public can't see the demands, the public can't tell a win from a performance. A negotiation with secret terms is just theater with better lighting.
  • Targeted duties. Hit the specific goods tied to the specific grievance. A scalpel pressures the other government; a grenade pressures your own shoppers.
  • Sunset clauses. Every duty expires unless re-authorized on evidence. Leverage with an expiration date is a negotiation. Leverage without one is a lifestyle — and lifestyles don't get reviewed.
  • Exemptions where America doesn't make the thing. Don't tax working people for buying goods with no domestic substitute. There's no American industry to protect there — that's just a tax on shoppers.
  • A recorded vote. Tariffs are taxes, and the Constitution says revenue bills start in the House. If the policy is worth doing, it's worth putting a name on. Watch who refuses to vote on it — that tells you who already knows how it polls when the prices hit.

None of that exists. So ask yourself what the policy is actually for — because a government serious about winning would build the scoreboard first, and nobody in Washington will touch one. A scoreboard ends the game. The game is the point.

Your distrust is data

This part is for the kid who's been priced out of the venue, the apartment, the grocery run — who said the game felt rigged years ago and got told that noticing it makes you the problem.

You were reading the incentives correctly the whole time. The kid working the door for cash understands this standoff better than the panel on cable news, because you live downstream of every decision those people make. Your wallet feels the tariff before the white paper is finished. Call it field research.

They need you mad enough to click and numb enough to never ask for the scoreboard. Don't give them numb. Be angry — just aim it upward, at the people holding the gun, not the people standing next to you at the register. This country was founded by ordinary people who read a tax bill and refused to pay it — saying the emperor's tariff has no clothes is the most American thing there is. So audit them the only way that can't be spun: what changed in your actual life? Not your feed. Your life.

Sources

Every factual claim in this autopsy traces to one of these. Don't take our word for any of it — that's the entire point of this site.

  • The 100% tariff authority. H.R. 5334, the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, signed September 18, 2026. It authorizes tariffs of up to 100% on the five largest purchasers of Russian oil and gas — China and India the obvious targets — and up to 500% on Russia itself. The tariffs are discretionary: nothing is imposed automatically. Passed the House 262–159 and the Senate 86–11. (White House signing statement, via RNA; Reuters, via Undercode News; CNBC, via Traders Union)
  • Who pays the tariff. The importer of record pays U.S. Customs at the dock; the foreign exporter pays Washington nothing. The burden then gets split between importers, retailers, and shoppers. (Brookings explainer, via Archyde)
  • The pass-through evidence. Amiti, Redding & Weinstein (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2019; updated 2020): nearly the entire cost of the 2018 U.S. tariffs was borne by U.S. consumers and importers, with almost complete pass-through into domestic prices — consistent with Fajgelbaum et al. (2019). Cavallo et al. found the pass-through nearly complete at the dock but only partial at retail, meaning some retailers quietly absorbed a slice. The U.S. International Trade Commission (March 2023) found U.S. importers bore nearly the full cost of the Section 232 and 301 tariffs it studied. (RePEc; LSE Business Review; USITC via AlphaTrader News)
  • Retaliation hit American exporters. China answered the 2018 tariffs with retaliatory duties of up to 25% on U.S. goods including soybeans. USDA estimates put soybean farmers' annualized losses at $9.4 billion — 71% of total U.S. agricultural export losses. U.S. soybean exports to China fell from $12.3 billion (2017) to $3.1 billion (2018). (American Soybean Association; The Dispatch)
  • The exclusion machine. Between 2018 and 2020, USTR received about 53,000 tariff-exclusion requests from American firms — and denied 87% of them, per the Government Accountability Office. (National Taxpayers Union summary of the GAO report)
  • Revenue bills start in the House. U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, Clause 1 (the Origination Clause).

The analysis and the incentives-mapping are ours. The facts are theirs. If you spot an error, corrections go on the front page.

Verdict: the cannon stays loaded because firing it wounds the shooter — and nobody will build the scoreboard that would prove whether the threat is working, because the threat is more useful than the proof. That's the whole story: a weapon too expensive to use, too useful to put down, and too profitable to ever audit. The receipts on who pays have been public for years — nobody in that chain gets to claim surprise. Watch what they do, not what they threaten. Check your receipts, not their headlines. Your wallet is the only honest witness in Washington.
