The Exceptionalism Blindfold

Gross federal debt has passed $40 trillion — roughly 122% of GDP. Net interest hit $1.05 trillion in fiscal 2026, the third-largest federal expense. The numbers are public. This is about the belief that keeps them from mattering: that the math doesn't apply to America.

By Jacob · Editor · October 19, 2026 · 10 min read

This month we autopsied two machines: the ratchet that keeps tightening around a constitutional right, and the fiscal machinery that turned the richest nation on earth into history's largest debtor. Both run in plain sight. Both have been running for decades.

So here is the prior question, the one underneath both: why doesn't anyone see it?

The numbers are published. People had every chance to read them — and chose the flattery instead. Chose the story that says the math doesn't apply to them. Ignorance can be fixed with information. Refusing to look is a decision, and decisions can be confronted. For decades, Americans chose not to look, because looking would mean giving up the story that the rules don't apply to us. And every officeholder in the country was happy to sell the blindfold, because a public that won't read ledgers never asks where the money went.

The problem is the belief that your love of country makes you exempt from math.

THE DISCOUNT RATE ON BAD NEWS

Here's the mechanism, stated plainly:

Belief: America is uniquely exceptional — not just different, but exempt.
Effect: Every piece of bad news about America gets run through a filter: but we're America, we'll be fine.
Payoff: The people operating the machines never face the ledgers. Accountability never arrives, because the audience has been taught that accountability is for other countries.

This is a discount rate. In finance, a discount rate shrinks the present value of future costs — a dollar of pain ten years out counts as fifty cents today. Exceptionalism does the same thing to political pain. Forty trillion dollars of debt goes in; 'we've always figured it out before' comes out. Interest payments pass defense spending; 'the dollar is the world's reserve currency' absorbs the shock. Each fact gets marked down at the door. Every flag-draped speech, every 'indispensable nation' address, every campaign ad with a sunrise is a fresh coat of paint on the blindfold — applied by people whose jobs depend on you never taking it off.

The belief doesn't have to be argued. It just has to be assumed — and it's comfortable to assume. It's absorbed in school, in speeches, in the way both parties describe America as the indispensable nation while the numbers rot underneath. Nobody holds a gun to anyone's head. The blindfold stays on because taking it off would mean admitting the flattery worked.

WHO BENEFITS FROM THE BLINDFOLD

Follow the incentives, as always. Who gains if the public keeps discounting bad news?

Incumbents of both parties. If America is inherently exceptional, then failures are blips, not systemic. A blip needs a speech. Systemic rot needs resignations — so Washington produces speeches, and the blindfold makes sure nobody asks for the resignations.

The defense establishment. Exceptionalism is the moral license for a military budget larger than the next six countries combined — about $954 billion in 2025. If America is uniquely virtuous, its wars are uniquely justified, and questioning the cost is treated as disloyalty. The flag gets draped over the budget so you can't see the price tag. The payoff is measured in appropriations.

The media and pundit class. Patriotic content sells. Reassurance sells. 'America is back,' 'America is strong' — products with a proven market, sold by outlets that need access to the very people the blindfold protects. Reassurance never gets you uninvited from the press briefing. Telling 330 million people their empire is mismanaged does — so most outlets don't.

Everyone in the chain gets paid — in votes, budgets, or ratings — for keeping the blindfold on. The mechanism runs on incentives.

WHAT HAMILTON ACTUALLY SAID

The standard story goes like this: the Founders took on national debt to show the world America's worth by paying it back, and modern leaders betrayed that vision by stopping.

Here's what Alexander Hamilton actually argued in his First Report on the Public Credit, delivered to Congress on January 9, 1790:

Hamilton wanted the new federal government to honor its debts — about $79 million — at full face value. His famous line was that 'the proper funding of the present debt, will render it a national blessing.' But the blessing had conditions, and the conditions are the whole point:

  1. Dedicated revenue. The plan pledged tariff and tonnage revenue, irrevocably, to a payment schedule. The debt was tied to real money coming in the door.
  2. Credible servicing. Pay on time, every time, and borrowing costs fall. After the plan passed, U.S. government securities tripled in value — the market believed the promise because the revenue was pledged behind it.
  3. Productive purpose. Serviced debt would free capital for agriculture and manufacturing. The debt was a tool for building the economy, not a substitute for one.

Hamilton's mechanism was: borrow against pledged revenue, service religiously, borrow cheaper next time, invest the difference. Break any link in that chain and the 'blessing' becomes a curse. Modern Washington kept the borrowing and broke everything else. There is no pledged revenue behind today's debt. There is no credible servicing plan. The money doesn't build — it services past borrowing.

Hamilton's sentence was properly funded debt is useful — and the word properly is worth trillions.

THE PAYDOWN: PROOF IT CAN BE DONE — WITH FINE PRINT

Here's the part the blindfold hides most carefully: America has already climbed out of a debt hole this deep. Once.

In 1946, after World War II, federal debt stood at 106% of GDP. By 1974 it was down to 23%. Eighty-three percentage points of GDP, erased in 28 years.

But honesty requires the fine print, because the fine print is the autopsy:

Economic research (Acalin and Ball, via the IMF and the National Bureau of Economic Research) dug into how the ratio fell. Growth helped, but growth alone wouldn't have done it — in their counterfactual, growth without the other factors only gets the ratio down to about 74%, not 23%. The rest came from three things:

  • Primary budget surpluses — the government routinely took in more than it spent, before interest.
  • Surprise inflation — rising prices silently shrank the real value of the debt. Bondholders got paid back in cheaper dollars. Inflation is a tax that never goes through Congress.
  • Pegged interest rates — before the 1951 Fed-Treasury Accord, rates were held down artificially, so the government borrowed cheap while inflation did the quiet work.

Read that list again. The 'success story' was built on surpluses (discipline), plus inflation and suppressed rates (a hidden tax on every saver and bondholder in the country). The debt was paid down, in part, by people who never voted for it and never got a receipt.

The fine print is the point of the autopsy: even America's greatest fiscal achievement had costs that were never put to a vote. The blindfold doesn't just hide failures. It hides the price tags on the successes.

THE REVERSAL: THE NUMBERS NOW

The ratio bottomed around 23% in the mid-1970s. Then it turned, and it has been climbing for more than four decades:

  • 1980: about 32% of GDP.
  • Today: gross federal debt has passed $40 trillion — roughly 122% of GDP. Debt held by the public (the stricter measure, what the government owes outside creditors) is around 100% of GDP, a line not crossed since World War II.
  • The deficit runs near $2 trillion a year — about 6% of GDP — during good times. Unemployment around 4%, solid growth. This is what the balance sheet looks like in peacetime prosperity.
  • Net interest — just the cost of carrying the debt — hit $1.05 trillion in fiscal year 2026, up 8% from the year before. It is now the third-largest item in the federal budget, behind Social Security and Medicare. It exceeds national defense. That's roughly $20 billion a week, or about $6,250 for every individual tax return filed. Twenty billion a week to creditors — paid by people who weren't in the room when the borrowing was chosen. Roughly 28% of it flows overseas: American tax dollars paying foreign creditors.
  • The Congressional Budget Office projects net interest rising from $1.05 trillion this year to $2.1 trillion by 2036 — nearly a fifth of all federal spending. That's the baseline. Current law.

Per the Joint Economic Committee's October 2026 figures: about $117,707 of debt for every American, about $298,606 per household. A newborn in Dallas arrives with a $117,707 share of the national debt. The borrowing was chosen before she existed; the bill arrived with her.

Now run each of those numbers through the blindfold. Forty trillion goes in; 'we've always figured it out' comes out. Interest exceeds defense; 'reserve currency' absorbs it. A trillion a year to creditors — roughly 28% of it flowing overseas — and the audience has been trained to hear it as background noise.

The numbers aren't hidden. They're discounted.

THE IMMUNITY QUESTION

Here's the premise: immunity to propaganda requires rethinking your own thought processes. This is what that looks like in practice.

The blindfold is installed by flattery: you're told your country is the greatest, your patriotism the truest, your system the envy of the world, and the price of that flattery is that you stop reading the ledgers. Propaganda works by making you feel the numbers don't apply to you.

Only you can take the blindfold off. That's the hard truth at the bottom of this month's work. We can show the gears — the ratchet, the fiscal machinery, the discount rate. We can publish the math, the history, the fine print. But the rethinking, the moment where a reader catches their own mind marking down bad news because we're America — that happens inside one head at a time, or it doesn't happen.

They will keep selling the flattery as long as you keep buying it. It's the most profitable product in American politics.

The machines run in plain sight. They always have. The only question is whether you're looking at them, or at the story about why you don't need to.
