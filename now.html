1. Shutdown reaches day 30 as the November food assistance cutoff approaches and two courts hear the SNAP cases

What happened: On October 30, the federal government shutdown reached its 30th day. Congress has not passed funding bills for fiscal year 2026, which began on October 1, or a continuing resolution to temporarily maintain funding. Democrats are withholding support for a continuing resolution to press for an extension of Affordable Care Act tax credits set to expire at the end of the year; open enrollment for 2026 coverage begins on November 1. The Agriculture Department has told states that no Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits will be issued on November 1, stating "the well has run dry." The program serves about 42 million people and costs about $8 billion a month. On October 27, attorneys general and governors from 25 states and the District of Columbia sued the Agriculture Department in federal court in Boston, arguing the department should use about $6 billion in contingency funds to pay November benefits. At a hearing on October 29, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani questioned the administration's position that the contingency fund cannot be used during a shutdown, noting that if the government cannot cover the full cost, there is a process for reducing benefits rather than suspending them entirely, and she said her ruling would apply nationwide rather than only in the states that sued. A second, similar challenge filed by a coalition of cities and nonprofit organizations was scheduled for a hearing before U.S. District Judge John McConnell Jr. in Rhode Island on October 30. Government lawyers argued that a contingency fund holding about $5 billion cannot legally be used to maintain SNAP during a lapse in appropriations, citing the Antideficiency Act.

Incentives:

  • Congress: the documented mechanism is the 60-vote Senate threshold for a continuing resolution, which lets a minority block funding until its condition is met; the stated Democratic condition is the health-care tax credit extension.
  • The Agriculture Department: the documented mechanisms are the hold-off instruction to states and the contingency-fund decision, which together determine whether November benefits are issued during the lapse.
  • The suing states and the cities and nonprofits: the documented mechanism is a federal lawsuit asking a judge to order the use of contingency funds, with the Boston hearing held October 29 and the Rhode Island hearing scheduled October 30.
  • Structural note: this edition reports the hearings and the positions argued, not a ruling on November benefits; the Boston hearing produced no reported order in the sources checked for this edition, and the Rhode Island hearing was scheduled for October 30.

Sources:

  • Associated Press reporting, October 29 — Boston hearing, Talwani questions, nationwide scope, Rhode Island hearing scheduled October 30
  • Associated Press, October 29 — Talwani statements, $5 billion contingency fund figure, administration Antideficiency Act argument
  • National Low Income Housing Coalition, October 27 — day count, premium increase, November 1 cutoff, 42 million recipients
  • Produce Market Guide, October 27 — states' lawsuit, $6 billion contingency fund

2. Trump and Xi meet in Busan and agree to a one-year trade truce: fentanyl tariffs halved, rare-earth controls paused, soybean purchases resumed

What happened: On October 30, U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping met in Busan, South Korea, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, their first face-to-face meeting since 2019. The meeting, held at Gimhae Air Base, lasted more than 90 minutes. Trump said tariffs on Chinese imports would be cut to 47 percent from 57 percent, by halving the tariff tied to fentanyl precursor drugs to 10 percent, in exchange for Beijing cracking down on the illicit fentanyl trade. China agreed to pause export controls on rare earths announced earlier in October, with China's commerce ministry stating the pause would last for a year, and to resume purchases of U.S. soybeans and other farm products, with Trump saying Xi authorized purchases starting immediately. The two sides also agreed to pause tit-for-tat port fees on shipping. Trump said China would begin the process of purchasing U.S. energy, including oil and gas from Alaska, and that he would travel to China in April before receiving Xi in the United States. Trump said he did not discuss Nvidia's Blackwell chip with Xi. U.S. negotiators had said on October 26 that they agreed a framework with China to avoid 100 percent U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods and defer China's export curbs on rare earths. No formal written deal was published with the meeting; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent indicated signatures could be exchanged the following week. Chinese export controls announced in April on seven rare earths, including gallium and germanium, remain in place, according to Bruegel's reading of the arrangement.

Incentives:

  • The United States: the documented mechanisms are the tariff cut, which lowers the average rate on Chinese imports from 57 percent to 47 percent, and the suspension of the planned 100 percent tariff escalation set for November 1.
  • China: the documented mechanisms are the one-year pause on the October export controls and the resumption of agricultural purchases, which restore a flow of rare earths and farm trade while keeping the April controls in place.
  • Both governments: the documented mechanism is the one-year term with annual renegotiation, which sets a dated point for the next round of bargaining rather than a permanent settlement.
  • Structural note: the arrangement was announced through statements and social media posts, not a published signed agreement, and implementation steps had not been taken when this edition was published.

Sources:

  • Reuters, October 30 — 47 percent from 57 percent, fentanyl tariff halved to 10 percent, rare-earth pause of one year, soybean purchases, port fees pause, meeting length
  • JD Supra, October 30 — MOFCOM confirmation, one-year suspension of enhanced controls, U.S. suspension of the affiliates rule, no formal action yet taken
  • Bruegel, October 30 — April controls remain in place, one-year expiration, Blackwell chip not lifted, April visit announced

3. Trump orders the Pentagon to resume nuclear weapons testing, the first such order since the 1992 moratorium

What happened: On October 30, while traveling to meet Xi Jinping in Busan, President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social that he had instructed the Department of War to start testing nuclear weapons on an equal basis with other countries, and that the process would begin immediately. The United States has not conducted a nuclear explosive test since 1992. Trump wrote that the United States has more nuclear weapons than any other country, that Russia is second, and that China is third but will reach parity within five years. The post did not specify whether the testing would involve nuclear explosive detonations, which are carried out by the National Nuclear Security Administration, or flight testing of nuclear-capable missiles, which is carried out by the Pentagon. The order followed Russia's announcements earlier in October of successful tests of the Burevestnik nuclear-powered cruise missile and the Poseidon nuclear-powered torpedo. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent defended the decision on October 30, saying it was needed for the United States to catch up. Trump declined to elaborate on the post when asked about it during his meeting with Xi.

Incentives:

  • The administration: the documented mechanism is a public instruction to the Department of War, issued minutes before the meeting with Xi, which states a testing program without specifying its form.
  • Russia and China: the documented mechanism is the sequence of announced Russian weapons tests earlier in October, which Trump cited in the post as the basis for testing on an equal basis.
  • Structural note: the form of testing remains unspecified in the public record, and no test date or location was announced when this edition was published.

Sources:

  • CNA, October 30 — Truth Social post text, 1992 moratorium, ambiguity between explosive and missile testing, Russian tests
  • Benzinga, October 30 — "equal basis" wording, first-term stockpile claim, Poseidon and Burevestnik tests, no comment at the bilateral meeting
  • Times of India, October 30 — Bessent defense, timing minutes before the Xi meeting

4. The Federal Reserve holds rates on October 28, then October 29 data shows 3.4 percent growth and 3.6 percent inflation

What happened: On October 28, the Federal Open Market Committee held the federal funds rate in a target range of 3.75 to 4 percent, with the interest rate paid on reserve balances maintained at 3.90 percent effective October 29. The vote was 10-2: Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented in favor of a quarter-point increase. The committee's statement said inflation remains elevated and economic activity is expanding at a strong pace, and that more information will help determine the policy stance needed for a return of inflation to the 2 percent goal. Chair Kevin Warsh declined to commit to a December move; the next decision is due in December. On October 29, one day after the decision, the Bureau of Economic Analysis released two reports the committee did not have in hand: the advance estimate of third-quarter gross domestic product, showing growth at a 3.4 percent annualized rate, and the September personal income and outlays report. The September report showed the personal consumption expenditures price index up 0.5 percent from August and 3.6 percent from a year earlier, up from 3.4 percent in August; excluding food and energy, the core index rose 0.3 percent on the month and 3.1 percent on the year. Personal income rose $54.5 billion, or 0.2 percent, and disposable personal income rose $52.7 billion, or 0.2 percent, while personal consumption expenditures rose $113.1 billion, or 0.5 percent, in current dollars. After adjusting for inflation, real consumer spending rose less than 0.1 percent, and real disposable personal income fell 0.3 percent. The personal saving rate was 3.8 percent. The advance GDP estimate is the first of three estimates for the quarter, with the second scheduled for November 25.

Incentives:

  • The Federal Reserve: the documented mechanism is the target range itself, which sets the level of short-term interest rates, and the dissent procedure, which records the two votes for a higher range without changing the outcome.
  • The Bureau of Economic Analysis: the documented mechanism is the release calendar, which placed both reports on October 29, one day after the committee's decision, so the September inflation reading could not inform the October vote.
  • Borrowers and savers: the documented mechanism is the pass-through from the federal funds range to market rates, which the reports measure indirectly through the saving rate and the gap between nominal and inflation-adjusted spending.
  • Structural note: the 3.6 percent figure is the September year-over-year PCE reading, while the 2.1 percent figure in the GDP release is the third-quarter annualized PCE rate; the two measure different periods and are not interchangeable.

Sources:

  • Federal Reserve, October 28 — implementation note, 3.90 percent reserve rate, 3.75 to 4 percent target range, 4.0 percent primary credit rate
  • Bureau of Economic Analysis, October 29 — personal income, disposable income, PCE, personal outlays, saving rate
  • Reuters, October 29 — PCE 0.5 percent monthly and 3.6 percent yearly, core 3.1 percent, GDP 3.4 percent, inflation-adjusted spending unchanged
  • Investopedia, October 29 — 10-2 vote context, gasoline and energy goods driving the monthly increase, September as the 67th month above the 2 percent goal

5. Israel's final results confirm no bloc holds a majority: Netanyahu 53, opposition 48, Arab-led parties 19

What happened: Results released by Israel's Central Elections Committee early on October 30 showed Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc winning 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot winning 48 seats, and Arab-led parties winning 19 seats. Sixty-one seats are needed for a majority. Likud emerged as the largest party with 27 seats, followed by Eisenkot's Yashar with 21 and Together with 12; the Joint List won 11 seats, the Democrats and Ra'am eight each, and Yisrael Beiteinu, United Torah Judaism, and Otzma Yehudit seven each, with Shas and Religious Zionism at six each. Final turnout was 77.9 percent, with 5,716,665 ballots cast. The outgoing Netanyahu coalition held 68 seats at its peak in the previous parliament. The results are scheduled to be submitted to President Isaac Herzog on November 4, after which Herzog will hold consultations with party representatives before deciding which lawmaker to task with forming a government; the 26th Knesset is scheduled to be sworn in on November 10. On October 29, Likud amplified claims that Arab-led parties had "stolen" the election, potentially with foreign assistance, after election committee head Supreme Court Justice Noam Sohlberg said serious suspicions of foreign interference had been brought before him and asked the Shin Bet security agency to investigate. The election commission said the investigation does not affect the final results and does not concern any particular sector of the population; Qatar denied the allegations, calling them ridiculous and dangerous. Netanyahu remains prime minister as caretaker during negotiations.

Incentives:

  • The blocs: the documented mechanism is coalition arithmetic in a 120-seat parliament — 53 plus 19 reaches 72, and 48 plus 19 reaches 67, so either bloc needs Arab-led party backing to reach 61, while Netanyahu has said it is forbidden to form a coalition with Arab parties and Eisenkot has indicated he is open, with conditions, to cooperating with them.
  • The caretaker government: Netanyahu stays in office during negotiations, which keeps executive authority with the incumbent while talks continue.
  • The election committee and the Shin Bet: the documented mechanism is the investigation into the foreign-interference suspicions, with findings requested within a week, running parallel to the coalition process without changing the submitted results.
  • Structural note: the results are complete but remain subject to minor corrections during routine verification before submission to the president on November 4.

Sources:

  • Associated Press, October 30 — 53-48-19 results, Likud claims, Sohlberg letter, Shin Bet statement, Qatar denial, Herzog consultations
  • New York Post, October 30 — per-party seat counts, 77.9 percent turnout, 5,716,665 ballots, November 4 submission, November 10 swearing-in
  • Associated Press, October 30 — loss of majority confirmed, weeks of negotiations ahead, Netanyahu remains in charge

6. Alaska ballot dispute widens: judge allows intervention on October 28, then a state inquiry and a House inquiry open on October 29

What happened: On October 28, U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason allowed the campaign committee of Democratic challenger Mary Peltola and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee to intervene in the Justice Department's lawsuit over Alaska's November 3 U.S. Senate election, and also permitted intervention by the League of Women Voters, Common Cause, and individual voters. The department had sued on October 22, asking the court to block certification of any result that may be impacted by voter confusion between incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan and challenger Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., pending an investigation into whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant for the incumbent. The Alaska Supreme Court had earlier directed the Division of Elections to include Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. on the ballot, and Alaska's Division of Elections disqualified him on June 15 before that ruling. On October 28, the New York Times reported, based on interviews with 12 people, text messages, and documents, that operatives aligned with Peltola's campaign and senior aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee worked for months to recruit Daniel J. Sullivan to the ballot. On October 28, Governor Mike Dunleavy directed the Department of Public Safety to examine possible violations of Alaska law and whether criminal charges or civil action are warranted. On October 29, House Oversight Chairman James Comer opened a congressional investigation and requested interviews for the following week, and the Justice Department filed an amended complaint adding the Times reporting and a Supremacy Clause count, asking Gleason to deny the three motions to dismiss as moot. No ruling on the motions to dismiss had been reported when this edition was published, and no finding of criminal wrongdoing has been made in any of these proceedings.

Incentives:

  • The court: the documented mechanism is the intervention order, which gives the campaign committees, voter organizations, and individual voters standing to argue for dismissal in a case filed against the state.
  • The state and Congress: the documented mechanisms are the governor's inquiry through the Department of Public Safety and the House Oversight inquiry, which run parallel to the federal lawsuit without themselves removing a candidate or changing a vote.
  • The Justice Department: the documented mechanism is the amended complaint, which adds a Supremacy Clause count and asks the court to treat the dismissal motions as moot.
  • Structural note: the recruitment account comes from the Times report and the inquiries it prompted; it is not a court finding, and the lawsuit's allegations of a deceptive candidacy remain allegations.

Sources:
